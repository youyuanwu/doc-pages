Discrete Symmetries: Parity and Time Reversal

Alongside the continuous symmetries whose generators give conservation laws, quantum mechanics has crucial discrete symmetries — most importantly parity (spatial inversion) and time reversal. These cannot be built up from infinitesimal transformations, and time reversal introduces a genuinely new structure: an antiunitary operator. Their consequences range from selection rules to the Kramers degeneracy of half-integer-spin systems.

1. Parity

Parity Π^ inverts spatial coordinates, Π^r^Π^−1=−r^, Π^p^​Π^−1=−p^​, while leaving angular momentum L^=r^×p^​ invariant (it is an axial vector). Since Π^2=1^, its eigenvalues are ±1 (even/odd parity). When [Π^,H^]=0 — true for any central potential — stationary states have definite parity: the spherical harmonics satisfy Π^Yℓm​=(−1)ℓYℓm​, so orbitals alternate even/odd with ℓ.

Parity yields powerful selection rules: a matrix element ⟨f∣O^∣i⟩ vanishes unless the parities multiply to that of O^. Since the electric-dipole operator r^ is odd, dipole transitions connect only states of opposite parity (Δℓ odd) — Laporte's rule (see time-dependent perturbation theory). Parity is conserved by the electromagnetic and strong interactions but violated by the weak interaction (Wu experiment, 1957) — one of the deepest asymmetries in nature.

2. Time Reversal Is Antiunitary

Time reversal Θ^ reverses motion: Θ^r^Θ^−1=r^ but Θ^p^​Θ^−1=−p^​ and Θ^J^Θ^−1=−J^. Consistency with the canonical commutator [x^,p^​]=iℏ forces Θ^ to also complex-conjugate scalars:

Θ^(i)Θ^−1=−i.

By Wigner's theorem a symmetry is unitary or antiunitary; time reversal is the physical realization of the antiunitary case. For spinless particles Θ^=K^ (complex conjugation) in the position basis, so time reversal simply sends ψ(r,t)→ψ∗(r,−t). Antiunitarity is why there is no conserved "time-reversal charge" — the continuous-symmetry ⇒ conservation-law argument does not apply to Θ^.

3. Kramers Degeneracy

For a particle with spin, Θ^=e−iπS^y​/ℏK^, and a direct computation gives

Θ^2=(−1)2s1^={+1^,−1^,​s integer,s half-integer.​

The half-integer case has a striking consequence — Kramers' theorem: in any time-reversal-invariant system with an odd number of half-integer-spin particles, every energy level is at least doubly degenerate, and no purely electric (time-reversal-even) perturbation can split it. A magnetic field, which breaks time reversal, is required to lift the degeneracy. This protects the two-fold degeneracy of electronic states in crystals without magnetic order and underlies the stability of Kramers doublets exploited in spin qubits and topological insulators.
