Addition of Angular Momenta

Real systems carry several angular momenta at once — orbital and spin of one particle, or the spins of several. Because the total angular momentum J^=J^1​+J^2​ generates rotations of the whole system, it is total J^, not the individual pieces, that is conserved and that labels states in a rotationally invariant problem. Combining angular momenta — decomposing a tensor product of representations into irreducibles — is therefore a constant practical need, from spin–orbit coupling to the singlet state to the coupling of quark spins.

1. Two Bases

Given two angular momenta j1​,j2​ with spaces of dimension (2j1​+1) and (2j2​+1), the product space has dimension (2j1​+1)(2j2​+1) and admits two natural bases:

  • Uncoupled ∣j1​,m1​⟩⊗∣j2​,m2​⟩: simultaneous eigenstates of J^12​,J^1z​,J^22​,J^2z​. Natural when the parts don't interact.
  • Coupled ∣j,m⟩: simultaneous eigenstates of J^12​,J^22​,J^2,J^z​ of the total J^. Natural when a rotationally invariant interaction (e.g. J^1​⋅J^2​) mixes the parts.

Note J^1z​ and J^z​ commute but J^1z​ and J^2 do not, so the two bases are genuinely different and related by a unitary change of basis.

2. The Clebsch–Gordan Series

The allowed total-j values follow from adding the maximal projections and stepping down. The result is the angular-momentum addition rule:

j=∣j1​−j2​∣, ∣j1​−j2​∣+1, …, j1​+j2​,​

each value occurring once, with m=m1​+m2​. In representation-theory language this is the decomposition of a tensor product of SU(2) irreps into a direct sum (the Clebsch–Gordan series):

D(j1​)⊗D(j2​)=j=∣j1​−j2​∣⨁j1​+j2​​D(j).

Dimensions check: ∑j​(2j+1)=(2j1​+1)(2j2​+1).

Example — two spin-21​: 21​⊗21​=1⊕0, i.e. a triplet (j=1, symmetric) and a singlet (j=0, antisymmetric). The singlet 2​1​(∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩) is the maximally entangled Bell state.

3. Clebsch–Gordan Coefficients

The change of basis is expanded with Clebsch–Gordan coefficients ⟨j1​m1​;j2​m2​∣jm⟩:

∣jm⟩=m1​+m2​=m∑​⟨j1​m1​;j2​m2​∣jm⟩∣j1​m1​⟩∣j2​m2​⟩.

They are real (in the standard Condon–Shortley convention), vanish unless m=m1​+m2​ and ∣j1​−j2​∣≤j≤j1​+j2​, and are computed by starting from the "stretched" state ∣j1​+j2​,j1​+j2​⟩=∣j1​j1​⟩∣j2​j2​⟩ and applying the lowering operator J^−​=J^1−​+J^2−​ repeatedly, orthogonalizing between the towers.

4. Spin–Orbit Coupling in Practice

The hydrogen fine structure term ∝L^⋅S^ is not diagonal in the uncoupled ∣mℓ​,ms​⟩ basis, but is diagonal in the coupled ∣j,mj​⟩ basis, because

L^⋅S^=21​(J^2−L^2−S^2)⇒⟨L^⋅S^⟩=2ℏ2​[j(j+1)−ℓ(ℓ+1)−s(s+1)].

For an electron (s=21​) with ℓ>0 this splits each level into j=ℓ±21​ — the fine-structure doublets. Coupling angular momenta is exactly the step that makes such perturbations tractable.

5. The Wigner–Eckart Theorem

The most powerful payoff of this machinery. A spherical tensor operator T^q(k)​ (a set transforming among themselves under rotation like angular momentum k) has matrix elements that factor into a Clebsch–Gordan coefficient and a single geometry-independent reduced matrix element:

⟨j′m′∣T^q(k)​∣jm⟩=⟨jm;kq∣j′m′⟩⟨j′∥T^(k)∥j⟩.

All the m-dependence is in the (known) Clebsch–Gordan factor; the physics sits in one number. This instantly yields selection rules (m′=m+q, ∣j−k∣≤j′≤j+k) and the relative intensities of spectral lines within a multiplet — the reason dipole transitions obey Δℓ=±1, Δm=0,±1 (see time-dependent perturbation theory).
