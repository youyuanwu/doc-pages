Multivariable Differentiation

The heart of the multivariable foundations (analysis spine). The right notion of derivative in several variables is not the collection of partial derivatives but the total (Fréchet) derivative — the best linear approximation, encoded by the Jacobian matrix. Getting this right makes the chain rule a statement about matrix multiplication and sets up the tangent-space picture that manifolds (topology-manifolds.md §3) take for granted.

References: Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis, ch. 9; Spivak, Calculus on Manifolds, ch. 2.

1. Partial and Directional Derivatives

For f:U→R, U⊆Rn open, the partial derivative in the j-th coordinate is

∂xj∂f​(a)=t→0lim​tf(a+tej​)−f(a)​,

and more generally the directional derivative along a unit vector v is Dv​f(a)=limt→0​tf(a+tv)−f(a)​. Warning: existence of all partials (even all directional derivatives) does not imply continuity. The function f(x,y)=xy/(x2+y2) (with f(0,0)=0) has both partials at the origin yet is discontinuous there. Partials alone are too weak; we need the total derivative.

2. The Total (Fréchet) Derivative

A map f:U→Rm is (totally) differentiable at a if there is a linear map Df(a):Rn→Rm with

h→0lim​∥h∥​f(a+h)−f(a)−Df(a)h​​=0.

Df(a) is the derivative (or differential) of f at a — the unique best linear approximation, exactly the multivariable form of the one-variable statement f(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+o(h) (differentiation.md §1). Differentiability at a implies continuity at a.

2.1 The Jacobian matrix

In standard coordinates Df(a) is represented by the Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives,

Df(a)=[∂xj∂fi​(a)]1≤i≤m1≤j≤n​​∈Rm×n.

If f is differentiable then all partials exist and Df(a) is this matrix; the converse needs a regularity hypothesis:

Sufficient condition (C1⇒ differentiable). If all partial derivatives exist and are continuous on U, then f is differentiable on U (such f is called continuously differentiable, f∈C1).

So in practice "the partials are continuous" is the checkable certificate of differentiability.

2.2 Gradient

For scalar f:U→R, Df(a) is a row vector; its transpose is the gradient

∇f(a)=(∂x1∂f​,…,∂xn∂f​)(a),Dv​f(a)=∇f(a)⋅v.

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent and is orthogonal to level sets — the geometric facts behind Lagrange multipliers and the vector-calculus operator ∇ (vector-calculus.md §2).

3. The Chain Rule

Chain Rule. If g:U→Rm is differentiable at a and f:V→Rp is differentiable at g(a), then f∘g is differentiable at a and D(f∘g)(a)=Df(g(a))∘Dg(a), i.e. the Jacobian of a composite is the product of Jacobian matrices.

Proof idea. Compose the two best-linear-approximations; the cross error terms are o(∥h∥) because Df is bounded (a linear map on Rn is Lipschitz). □

This is the cleanest justification for the linear-algebra packaging of the derivative: the messy multivariable chain rule ∂xk∂​(f∘g)=∑j​∂yj∂f​∂xk∂gj​ is just the (i,k) entry of a matrix product.

4. Higher Derivatives and the Symmetry of Mixed Partials

Second partials ∂2f/∂xi∂xj assemble into the Hessian matrix Hf(a)=[∂2f/∂xi∂xj].

Clairaut–Schwarz Theorem. If the second partials of f are continuous on U (i.e. f∈C2), then mixed partials commute: ∂xi∂xj∂2f​=∂xj∂xi∂2f​.

Hence the Hessian is symmetric. This symmetry is exactly the algebraic fact d2=0 in disguise — it is what makes the exterior derivative well-defined and what gives curlgrad=0 and divcurl=0 (differential-forms.md §4).

5. Taylor's Theorem in Several Variables

For f∈Ck+1 near a,

f(a+h)=∣α∣≤k∑​α!Dαf(a)​hα+Rk​(h),Rk​(h)=O(∥h∥k+1),

in multi-index notation. The second-order expansion

f(a+h)=f(a)+∇f(a)⋅h+21​h⊤Hf(a)h+o(∥h∥2)

drives the second-derivative test: at a critical point (∇f=0), a positive-definite Hessian gives a local min, negative-definite a local max, indefinite a saddle. This is the one-variable Taylor theorem (differentiation.md §6) propagated along rays.

6. The Mean Value Inequality

The exact one-variable MVT (f(b)−f(a)=f′(ξ)(b−a)) has no vector-valued analogue (there may be no single ξ for all components). The usable replacement is an inequality:

Mean Value Inequality. If f:U→Rm is C1 and the segment [a,a+h]⊆U, then ∥f(a+h)−f(a)∥≤(supt∈[0,1]​>∥Df(a+th)∥)∥h∥.

This bound — a derivative controlling an increment — is what powers the convergence estimates in the contraction-mapping proof of the inverse function theorem (inverse-implicit.md).

7. Connection to Tangent Spaces

The derivative-as-linear-map is the flat model of the manifold notion of differential. For a smooth map F:M→N between manifolds, the pushforward dFp​:Tp​M→TF(p)​N is, in charts, precisely the Jacobian D(ψ∘F∘ϕ−1) of this page (topology-manifolds.md §3). Everything here is the local coordinate computation that the intrinsic manifold language packages coordinate-free.
