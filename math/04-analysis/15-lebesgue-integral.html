The Lebesgue Integral

With measure theory in hand we can define an integral that repairs the two structural defects of the Riemann integral (riemann-integral.md §7): its integrable class is too small (the Dirichlet function is excluded), and it does not commute with pointwise limits. The Lebesgue integral fixes both, at the price of the measure-theoretic apparatus, and it is the integral used everywhere downstream — the Lp spaces of functional analysis, the expectation of probability, and the densities that Radon–Nikodym produces in §7.

Throughout, (X,A,μ) is a measure space and "measurable" means A-measurable.

References: Rudin, Real and Complex Analysis, ch. 1–3, 6; Folland, Real Analysis, ch. 2–3.

1. Slice the Range, Not the Domain

Riemann partitions the domain and asks how much f varies over each piece — which is why wild oscillation (the Dirichlet function) is fatal. Lebesgue partitions the range: for each layer [c,c+Δc) ask how big is the set where f takes values in that layer, and add c⋅μ(that set). Wild oscillation costs nothing; the only requirement is that the level sets be measurable, which is precisely the definition of a measurable function (measure-theory.md §7).

The construction proceeds in three steps — simple → non-negative → general — each defined so that the previous one is a special case.

2. Simple Functions

A simple function is a measurable s:X→[0,∞) with finite range; writing its distinct values c1​,…,cn​ and Ai​=s−1(ci​) gives the canonical form s=∑i=1n​ci​1Ai​​ with the Ai​ disjoint and measurable.

Definition. ∫X​sdμ:=i=1∑n​ci​μ(Ai​)>∈[0,∞], with the convention 0⋅∞=0.

The convention is what makes ∫0dμ=0 on a space of infinite measure, and it is forced if the theory is to be consistent. One checks directly that the value is independent of the representation, that ∫ is additive and positively homogeneous on simple functions, and that s≤t⟹∫s≤∫t.

3. Non-Negative Functions

Definition. For measurable f:X→[0,∞],

∫X​fdμ:=sup{∫X​sdμ:s simple, 0≤s≤f}.

The supremum always exists in [0,∞], so every non-negative measurable function has an integral — possibly +∞. This is a real difference from the Riemann theory, where integrability is a nontrivial hypothesis; here the hypothesis reappears only when we ask for a finite value.

By simple approximation (measure-theory.md §7) there are simple sk​↑f, and the next theorem says the sup is a limit along any such sequence.

4. The Convergence Theorems

These three results are the reason the Lebesgue integral exists. All are statements about exchanging lim and ∫, and all fail for the Riemann integral.

Monotone Convergence Theorem (MCT). If 0≤f1​≤f2​≤⋯ are measurable and fk​↑f pointwise, then ∫fk​dμ↑∫fdμ.

Proof sketch. "≤" is monotonicity. For "≥", fix simple s≤f and c∈(0,1), and set Ek​={fk​≥cs}. Then Ek​↑X, and continuity of μ from below (measure-theory.md §3) gives ∫fk​≥c∫Ek​​s→c∫s. Let c↑1 and take the sup over s. □

Note where the hypotheses are consumed: countable additivity enters exactly once, as continuity from below. MCT is the countable additivity of μ transposed to functions — which is why finitely additive measures have no useful integration theory (finite-additivity.md).

Corollary (countable additivity of the integral). For measurable fk​≥0, ∫∑k​fk​dμ=∑k​∫fk​dμ. Sums and integrals of non-negative terms always commute.

Fatou's Lemma. For measurable fk​≥0, ∫kliminf​fk​dμ≤kliminf​∫fk​dμ.

Proof. Apply MCT to gk​=infj≥k​fj​↑liminffk​, noting gk​≤fj​ for j≥k. □

The inequality can be strict: the "escaping mass" examples fk​=k1(0,1/k)​ (mass concentrates) and fk​=1[k,k+1]​ (mass escapes to infinity) both have liminffk​=0 pointwise but ∫fk​=1. Fatou is the correct one-sided statement in general; equality needs a dominating hypothesis.

Dominated Convergence Theorem (DCT). If fk​→f pointwise a.e. and there is a dominating g∈L1(μ) (i.e. ∫gdμ<∞) with ∣fk​∣≤g a.e. for all k, then f∈L1 and

k→∞lim​∫X​fk​dμ=∫X​fdμ,indeed∫∣fk​−f∣dμ→0.

Proof. Apply Fatou to 2g−∣fk​−f∣≥0. □

Both escaping-mass examples above violate domination (any g would need ∫g=∞), which is exactly what the theorem asks for. DCT is the workhorse: differentiating under the integral sign, continuity of Fourier transforms, and the completeness of Lp all reduce to it.

5. General and Complex-Valued Functions

For measurable f:X→R write f=f+−f− with f±=max(±f,0)≥0.

Definition. f is integrable (f∈L1(μ)) if ∫f+,∫f−><∞, equivalently ∫∣f∣dμ<∞; then ∫fdμ=>∫f+dμ−∫f−dμ. Complex f is integrable iff ∣f∣ is, with ∫f=∫Ref+i∫Imf.

The Lebesgue integral is absolute: f integrable ⟺ ∣f∣ integrable. Conditionally convergent objects such as ∫0∞​xsinx​dx are therefore not Lebesgue integrals but improper limits — a genuine expressive loss relative to the Riemann/gauge theories, and the reason improper integrals keep their own notation in physics texts.

Basic properties: linearity on L1, monotonicity, the triangle inequality ∣∫f∣≤∫∣f∣, and

Proposition. ∫X​∣f∣dμ=0⟺f=0 a.e. Consequently the integral does not distinguish functions equal a.e.

6. Comparison with Riemann

Theorem. If f is Riemann integrable on [a,b] then it is Lebesgue integrable with respect to λ1 and the two integrals agree.

So nothing computed in riemann-integral.md or multiple-integrals.md changes value; the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus remains the practical evaluation tool.

Theorem (Lebesgue's criterion). A bounded f:[a,b]→R is Riemann integrable iff its set of discontinuities is λ1-null.

Proof sketch. Define the oscillation oscf​(x)=limδ→0​diamf(Bδ​(x)); f is continuous at x iff oscf​(x)=0, and Dε​={oscf​≥ε} is compact. If the discontinuity set D=⋃k​D1/k​ is null, each Dε​ is null and compact, hence coverable by finitely many intervals of small total length; on the complement the oscillation is <ε, and splitting a partition accordingly gives U(f,P)−L(f,P)<ε(b−a+osc), the Riemann criterion. Conversely, if λ1(Dε​)>0 for some ε, every partition has U−L≥ελ1(Dε​)>0. □

This discharges the IOU left open in riemann-integral.md §3 and multiple-integrals.md §2. The Dirichlet function 1Q​ is discontinuous everywhere, hence not Riemann integrable, but as a function vanishing a.e. it has Lebesgue integral 0 — the standard illustration that the Lebesgue class is strictly larger.

7. Densities: Radon–Nikodym

Integration produces new measures from old, and the converse question — which measures arise this way — is answered completely.

Definition. ν is absolutely continuous with respect to μ (ν≪μ) if μ(A)=0⟹ν(A)=0. It is singular (ν⊥μ) if the two are carried by disjoint sets.

Theorem (Radon–Nikodym). If μ,ν are σ-finite and ν≪μ, there is a measurable h≥0, unique up to μ-a.e. equality, with

ν(A)=∫A​hdμfor all A∈A.

h=dμdν​ is the Radon–Nikodym derivative.

Theorem (Lebesgue decomposition). Any σ-finite ν splits uniquely as ν=νac​+νs​ with νac​≪μ and νs​⊥μ.

The slick proof of Radon–Nikodym (von Neumann's) runs through the Riesz representation theorem in the Hilbert space L2(μ+ν) — see hilbert-spaces.md. Two downstream uses fix its importance here:

  • Probability densities. A distribution on R has a density p exactly when it is ≪λ1, and then p=dν/dλ1 (random-variables.md). Distributions with atoms, or on the Cantor set, are the singular part.
  • Conditional expectation. E[X∣G] is defined as a Radon–Nikodym derivative of a signed measure restricted to G (conditional-expectation.md).

8. Lp Spaces

Definition. For 1≤p<∞, ∥f∥p​=(∫∣f∣pdμ)1/p and Lp(μ) is the set of measurable f with ∥f∥p​<∞, modulo equality a.e. For p=∞, ∥f∥∞​ is the essential supremum.

Quotienting by a.e. equality is not fastidiousness — without it ∥⋅∥p​ is only a seminorm (§5's proposition), and the space is not normed.

Hölder. ∥fg∥1​≤∥f∥p​∥g∥q​ for p1​+q1​=1. Minkowski. ∥f+g∥p​≤∥f∥p​+∥g∥p​ — the triangle inequality. Riesz–Fischer. Lp(μ) is complete: a Banach space, and L2 a Hilbert space.

Proof of completeness (sketch). Given a Cauchy sequence, extract a subsequence with ∥fk+1​−fk​∥p​≤2−k; the series ∑∣fk+1​−fk​∣ has finite Lp norm by MCT and Minkowski, hence converges a.e.; DCT identifies the limit and gives convergence in norm. □

Completeness is exactly the property that functional analysis assumes when it lists Lp among the standard Banach spaces, and the fact that L2 is a Hilbert space is what makes it the state space of quantum mechanics.

Convergence in Lp vs. a.e. Neither implies the other: the "typewriter" sequence of indicators of [j/2k,(j+1)/2k] tends to 0 in L1 but nowhere pointwise, while k1(0,1/k)​→0 pointwise but not in L1. What is true: Lp convergence implies a.e. convergence along a subsequence — the extraction step in the proof above. The systematic comparison of convergence modes is convergence-modes.md.

9. Where This Is Used

  • Expectation — E[X]=∫Ω​XdP; MCT/Fatou/DCT reappear verbatim as the convergence theorems of probability.
  • Conditional expectation — Radon–Nikodym.
  • Normed and Banach spaces, Hilbert spaces — Lp, L2.
