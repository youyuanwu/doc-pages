Multiple Integrals

The integration theory of several variables (analysis spine). We extend the Riemann integral (riemann-integral.md) to functions on boxes and Jordan-measurable regions of Rn, prove Fubini's theorem (iterated integration) and the change-of-variables formula with its Jacobian determinant. These are the tools that compute the volume, flux, and circulation integrals appearing in the integral theorems.

References: Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis, ch. 10; Spivak, Calculus on Manifolds, ch. 3.

1. Integration over Boxes

A box (rectangle) is R=[a1​,b1​]×⋯×[an​,bn​]; it plays the role of the domain of integration, the Rn analogue of the interval [a,b]. The integrand is a bounded function f:R→R defined on that box — so R is where f lives and f supplies a value f(x) at each point x∈R. A partition P of R is a choice, for each edge i, of a one-dimensional partition

ai​=ti,0​<ti,1​<⋯<ti,ki​​=bi​.

These grids together cut R into closed subboxes: for any index tuple (j1​,…,jn​) with 1≤ji​≤ki​,

Q=i=1∏n​[ti,ji​−1​, ti,ji​​].

The volume (or n-dimensional content) of such a subbox is the product of its edge lengths,

vol(Q)=i=1∏n​(ti,ji​​−ti,ji​−1​)≥0,

and this is likewise the definition of vol(R)=∏i=1n​(bi​−ai​) for the box itself. The subboxes have pairwise disjoint interiors, cover R, and their volumes are additive: ∑Q​vol(Q)=vol(R) (each factor bi​−ai​=∑ji​​(ti,ji​​−ti,ji​−1​) telescopes, and the product of sums expands to the sum over tuples). For bounded f:R→R, define lower and upper Darboux sums exactly as in one variable,

L(f,P)=Q∑​(Qinf​f)vol(Q),U(f,P)=Q∑​(Qsup​f)vol(Q),

the sum running over all subboxes Q of P. f is integrable if supP​L=infP​U, the common value being

∫R​f=∫R​f(x)dx1⋯dxn.

The Riemann criterion (U(f,P)−L(f,P)<ε) and the proof that continuous functions on a box are integrable (via uniform continuity on the compact box, metric-spaces.md §5) carry over verbatim from riemann-integral.md.

2. Jordan Measure and General Regions

To integrate over non-box regions E, extend f by 0 outside E and integrate f1E​ over a big box. This works when the boundary is negligible.

  • A set has (Jordan) measure zero if it can be covered by finitely many boxes of arbitrarily small total volume. (Lebesgue measure zero allows countably many — the distinction the integrability criterion below needs; both are made precise in measure-theory.md §§3, 6.)
  • E is Jordan measurable if ∂E has measure zero; then its volume is vol(E)=∫1E​.

Lebesgue's criterion. A bounded f on a box is Riemann integrable iff its discontinuity set has (Lebesgue) measure zero. Proved in lebesgue-integral.md §6.

Smooth hypersurfaces (graphs of C1 functions, regular level sets from inverse-implicit.md) have measure zero, so regions bounded by them — disks, balls, the solid regions in the divergence theorem — are Jordan measurable and continuous integrands over them are integrable.

3. Fubini's Theorem

Fubini's Theorem. If f is continuous (or bounded and integrable with a measure-zero discontinuity set) on R=A×B with A⊆>Rp, B⊆Rq boxes, then ∫R​f=∫A​(∫B​f(x,y)dy)dx=∫B​(∫A​f(x,y)dx)dy.

Proof (Darboux sums — click to expand).

Assume f continuous (so every inner integral below exists; the general case is handled in the remark that follows). Write points of R as (x,y) with x∈A, y∈B, and set the inner integral

g(x)=∫B​f(x,y)dy.

We show g is integrable on A with ∫A​g=∫R​f; the other order is identical by symmetry.

Step 1 — pair up partitions. Let PA​ partition A into subboxes S and PB​ partition B into subboxes T. Their product P=PA​×PB​ partitions R into subboxes S×T with vol(S×T)=vol(S)vol(T), so

L(f,P)=S∑​T∑​(S×Tinf​f)vol(T)vol(S).

Step 2 — bound the inner sum by g. Fix a subbox S and any point x∈S. Since {x}×T⊆S×T, we have infS×T​f≤infy∈T​f(x,y), hence

T∑​(S×Tinf​f)vol(T)≤T∑​(y∈Tinf​f(x,y))vol(T)=L(f(x,⋅),PB​)≤∫B​f(x,y)dy=g(x).

This holds for every x∈S, so the left-hand side (independent of x) is a lower bound for g on S, giving ∑T​(infS×T​f)vol(T)≤infS​g.

Step 3 — squeeze. Multiplying by vol(S) and summing over S,

L(f,P)≤S∑​(Sinf​g)vol(S)=L(g,PA​).

The mirror argument with suprema gives U(g,PA​)≤U(f,P). Combining with the trivial L(g,PA​)≤U(g,PA​),

L(f,P)≤L(g,PA​)≤U(g,PA​)≤U(f,P).

Because f is integrable, supP​L(f,P)=infP​U(f,P)=∫R​f; the outer two terms squeeze together, forcing L(g,PA​) and U(g,PA​) to the same limit. Hence g is integrable on A and ∫A​g=∫R​f. □

The general (integrable) case. If f is merely bounded and integrable, the inner integral ∫B​f(x,⋅) need not exist for every x. Replace g by the lower and upper inner integrals g​(x)=∫B​​>f(x,⋅) and g​(x)=∫B​​f(x,⋅). Steps 1–3 give L(f,P)≤L(g​,PA​)≤U(g​,PA​)≤U(f,P), so both g​ and g​ are integrable on A with integral ∫R​f; since g​≤g​ and their integrals agree, g​=>g​ except on a measure-zero set, and the iterated integral equals ∫R​f. (This is why the hypothesis is stated as "integrable," not just "each slice integrable.")

A multiple integral is computed as iterated one-variable integrals, and the

order may be swapped. For a region described as E={(x,y):x∈[a,b], φ(x)≤y≤ψ(x)} ("type I"), this gives ∫E​f=∫ab​∫φ(x)ψ(x)​f(x,y)dydx. Fubini is the practical engine for every multiple integral and is invoked repeatedly in the proofs of Green's and the divergence theorem (integral-theorems.md).

Hypotheses matter. Without integrability/absolute-integrability the iterated integrals can differ. The classic counterexample ∫01​∫01​>(x2+y2)2x2−y2​dydx=4π​=−4π​= (other order) shows Fubini fails for non-integrable integrands.

4. Change of Variables

Change-of-Variables Theorem. Let g:U→V be a C1 diffeomorphism between open sets in Rn (bijective, with C1 inverse), and let f be integrable on V. Then ∫V​f(y)dy=∫U​f(g(x))​detDg(x)​dx.

The factor ∣detDg∣ — the Jacobian determinant — is the local volume scaling of the map: Dg sends an infinitesimal box of volume dx to a parallelepiped of volume ∣detDg∣dx (determinant = oriented volume of the image of the unit cube). This is the n-dimensional generalization of the substitution rule's φ′ factor (riemann-integral.md §6), with ∣⋅∣ appearing because unoriented volume is intrinsically positive.

Proof idea. Locally g is approximated by the linear map Dg(a); for linear maps the determinant volume-scaling is elementary, and a partition-of-unity / covering argument with the inverse function theorem (inverse-implicit.md) globalizes it. □

Standard cases.

  • Polar (x,y)=(rcosθ,rsinθ): ∣detDg∣=r, so dxdy=rdrdθ.
  • Spherical (r,θ,ϕ): ∣detDg∣=r2sinθ, so dV=r2sinθdrdθdϕ.
  • Cylindrical (r,θ,z): ∣detDg∣=r.

5. Improper and Parameter-Dependent Integrals

For unbounded regions or integrands, define multiple integrals as limits over exhausting compact sets; for nonnegative integrands the limit is independent of the exhaustion. Differentiation under the integral sign (Leibniz rule): if f and ∂f/∂t are continuous, then dtd​∫A​f(x,t)dx=∫A​∂t∂f​(x,t)dx. These reductions all ultimately rest on the uniform-convergence theorems of sequences-series.md.

6. Orientation: A Preview

The change-of-variables formula carries an absolute value ∣detDg∣ because it measures unoriented volume. The integral theorems, by contrast, are sensitive to orientation (which way a boundary is traversed, which way a normal points), and there the signed determinant detDg is what appears. Reconciling these — unsigned volume vs signed orientation — is precisely the role of differential forms (differential-forms.md), where integration is defined to transform by detDg (not its absolute value) and orientation is built in from the start.

7. Where this page is used
