Expectation

Expectation is the Lebesgue integral against P, and every property of it is a property of the integral proved in lebesgue-integral.md. What this page adds is the probabilistic layer: the change-of-variables formula that lets one compute with laws instead of sample spaces, the standard inequalities, the transform methods (moment- and characteristic functions) that drive the central limit theorem, and the concentration bounds that make randomized algorithms work (complexity-theory.md §7).

References: Williams, Probability with Martingales, ch. 5–6; Durrett, Probability, §1.6, 3.3; Boucheron–Lugosi–Massart, Concentration Inequalities.

1. Definition

Definition. For a random variable X on (Ω,F,P),

E[X]:=∫Ω​XdP,

defined whenever the Lebesgue integral is: always for X≥0 (value in [0,∞]), and for general X when E∣X∣<∞, i.e. X∈L1(P).

The convergence theorems transfer verbatim and are used under their probabilistic names:

  • Monotone convergence. 0≤Xk​↑X⟹E[Xk​]↑E[X].
  • Fatou. E[liminfXk​]≤liminfE[Xk​] for Xk​≥0.
  • Dominated convergence. Xk​→X a.s., ∣Xk​∣≤Y with E[Y]<∞ ⟹E[Xk​]→E[X].
  • Bounded convergence. The special case ∣Xk​∣≤c — available here but not in general measure theory, because P(Ω)=1 makes constants integrable.

That last item is the one genuine simplification probability enjoys: on a probability space, L∞⊆Lp⊆L1 for all p, and ∥X∥p​ is non-decreasing in p (Jensen). Nothing of the sort holds on R with λ1.

2. Computing with the Law

Theorem (change of variables). For Borel g:R→R with g≥0 or g(X)∈L1,

E[g(X)]=∫Ω​g(X(ω))dP(ω)=∫R​g(x)dPX​(x).

Proof. True for g=1B​ by the definition of the pushforward PX​(B)=P(X∈B); extend by linearity to simple g, by monotone convergence to g≥0, and by decomposition to general g. □

This is why the sample space disappears (random-variables.md §5). In the two standard cases:

E[g(X)]=k∑​g(xk​)P(X=xk​)(discrete),E[g(X)]=∫R​g(x)p(x)dx(density p).

Both are instances of one theorem, which is the practical dividend of the measure-theoretic definition: elementary treatments need two separate theories.

Tail formula (layer cake). For X≥0, E[X]=∫0∞​P(X>t)dt, from Tonelli applied to 1{t<X(ω)}​ on Ω×>(0,∞) (product-measures.md §3). For integer-valued X≥0 this reads E[X]=∑k≥1​P(X≥k).

3. Moments and Variance

Definitions. The k-th moment is E[Xk]; the variance is Var(X)=E[(X−EX)2]=E[X2]−(EX)2; the covariance is Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y].

L2 is a Hilbert space (hilbert-spaces.md), and covariance is its inner product on centred variables. This is not an analogy: uncorrelated means orthogonal, Var is the squared norm, and bilinearity plus Pythagoras gives

Var(i=1∑n​Xi​)=i=1∑n​Var(Xi​)for uncorrelated Xi​.

The geometric reading pays off again in conditional-expectation.md, where conditioning is literally orthogonal projection.

Moments need not exist. The Cauchy law has no mean; the t-distribution with 2 degrees of freedom has a mean but no variance. Finiteness of moments is a real hypothesis, and the limit theorems say exactly which ones they need.

4. Inequalities

Markov. For X≥0 and t>0: P(X≥t)≤tE[X]​.

Proof. t1{X≥t}​≤X; take expectations. □

Everything else in this section is Markov applied to a cleverly chosen function.

Chebyshev. P(∣X−EX∣≥t)≤t2Var(X)​ — Markov applied to (X−EX)2.

Jensen. For convex φ with X,φ(X)∈L1: φ(E[X])≤E[φ(X)].

Proof. A convex function has a supporting line at E[X]: φ(x)≥φ(EX)+c(x−EX); take expectations. □

Jensen gives ∣EX∣≤E∣X∣, the monotonicity of Lp norms, and the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality as special cases.

Cauchy–Schwarz. ∣E[XY]∣≤∥X∥2​∥Y∥2​, with equality iff X,Y are a.s. proportional — the L2 inner-product inequality (lebesgue-integral.md §8), whence ∣Cov(X,Y)∣≤σX​σY​ and the correlation coefficient lies in [−1,1].

5. Generating Functions and Characteristic Functions

Definitions. The moment generating function is MX​(t)=E[etX] (possibly +∞), and the characteristic function is

φX​(t)=E[eitX]=∫R​eitxdPX​(x),

i.e. the Fourier transform of the law.

φX​ is defined for every law and every t — ∣eitX∣=1, so the integral converges absolutely — which is exactly why it, and not MX​, is the right tool. It is uniformly continuous, φX​(0)=1, ∣φX​∣≤1, and φaX+b​(t)=eitbφX​(at).

Uniqueness / inversion (Lévy). φX​ determines PX​. If φX​∈L1, the law has a bounded continuous density p(x)=2π1​∫e−itxφX​(t)dt.

Independence. X⊥Y⟹φX+Y​=φX​φY​ (independence.md §4). Convolution of laws becomes multiplication of transforms.

Moments. If E∣X∣n<∞ then φX​ is n times differentiable and φX(k)​(0)=ikE[Xk] — so a Taylor expansion of φX​ near 0 encodes the low-order moments, which is precisely how the CLT proof extracts a Gaussian.

Lévy continuity theorem. Xk​⇒X (in distribution) ⟺ φXk​​(t)→φX​(t) pointwise, provided the limit is continuous at 0. This converts a statement about measures into a statement about ordinary functions and is the engine of the CLT.

The moment problem is the caveat on the parallel theory of moments: a law is not in general determined by its moments (the lognormal has moment-identical companions). Determinacy needs a growth condition — e.g. Carleman's, or finiteness of MX​ near 0. Characteristic functions have no such defect.

6. Concentration

Chebyshev gives decay t−2; for sums of bounded independent variables the truth is exponential, and the improvement is what makes randomized computation practical.

Chernoff method. For any λ>0, P(S≥t)=P(eλS≥eλt)≤e−λtE[eλS], then optimize over λ. Markov plus the MGF.

Hoeffding's inequality. Let X1​,…,Xn​ be independent with ai​≤Xi​≤bi​ a.s., and S=∑i​Xi​. Then for t>0

P(∣S−E[S]∣≥t)≤2exp(∑i​(bi​−ai​)2−2t2​).

Proof sketch. Hoeffding's lemma bounds E[eλ(Xi​−EXi​)]≤eλ2(bi​−ai​)2/8 by convexity; independence multiplies the MGFs (§5); optimize λ in the Chernoff bound. □

Chernoff bound for Bernoulli sums. If S∼Bin(n,p) with μ=np, then P(S≥(1+δ)μ)≤e−δ2μ/(2+δ) and P(S≤(1−δ)μ)≤e−δ2μ/2.

Amplification — the complexity-theory use. A BPP machine accepts with probability ≥2/3 on yes-instances and ≤1/3 on no-instances (complexity-theory.md §7). Run it n times independently and take the majority: the number of correct runs is a sum of i.i.d. Bernoulli(2/3) variables, and Hoeffding with t=n/6 bounds the probability of a wrong majority by 2e−n/18. So poly(n) repetitions drive the error to 2−poly(n), which is why the constant 2/3 in the definition of BPP is arbitrary. That is the whole content of "the error can be amplified".

The distinction to keep: Chebyshev needs only a variance and is tight for heavy-tailed variables; exponential bounds need boundedness (or a sub-Gaussian / sub-exponential tail hypothesis) and are what fail for the Cauchy law.

7. Where This Is Used

  • Modes of convergence and laws of large numbers — Chebyshev gives the weak law in three lines.
  • The central limit theorem — §5's characteristic functions and the continuity theorem.
  • Conditional expectation — the L2 geometry of §3.
  • Computational complexity theory — §6's amplification.
