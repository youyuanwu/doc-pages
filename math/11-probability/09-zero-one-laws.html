Zero–One Laws

Some events have probability 0 or 1 for structural reasons — no computation with the underlying distribution is needed, only the observation that the event does not depend on any finite amount of the data. These results are the sharpest illustration of a recurring theme: a great deal is settled by the shape of the measure rather than by its values.

References: Durrett, Probability, §2.5; Kallenberg, Foundations, ch. 3; Williams, Probability with Martingales, ch. 4, 14.

1. Tail Events

Let X1​,X2​,… be random variables and Fn​=σ(Xn​,Xn+1​,…) the σ-algebra generated by the sequence from n onwards.

Definition. The tail σ-algebra is T=n=1⋂∞​Fn​. Its members are tail events: events unaffected by altering any finite number of the Xi​.

Examples of tail events:

  • {∑k​Xk​ converges} — changing finitely many terms cannot change convergence;
  • {Xk​∈B infinitely often};
  • {limk​Xˉn​ exists} and {limsupn​Xˉn​≤c} — the averages are not tail-measurable individually, but their limiting behaviour is, since finitely many terms contribute O(1/n).

Not tail events: {X1​=0}, {∑k​Xk​≤3} (the value of a convergent sum does depend on early terms).

2. Kolmogorov's Zero–One Law

Theorem. If X1​,X2​,… are independent, then every tail event has probability 0 or 1, and every T-measurable random variable is a.s. constant.

Proof. Let A∈T. For each n, A∈Fn+1​, which is independent of σ(X1​,…,Xn​); so A is independent of the π-system ⋃n​σ(X1​,…,Xn​), hence — by the π–λ argument of independence.md §2 — independent of the σ-algebra it generates, namely σ(X1​,X2​,…)⊇T. So A is independent of itself: P(A)=P(A∩A)=P(A)2, forcing P(A)∈{0,1}. □

The self-independence trick is the whole proof, and it is worth pausing on: the tail σ-algebra is simultaneously inside the sequence's σ-algebra and independent of every finite piece of it, and those two facts are only compatible if it is trivial.

Consequences, all immediate:

  • ∑k​Xk​ converges a.s. or diverges a.s. — never in between.
  • limsupn​Xˉn​ is an a.s. constant (possibly ±∞). This is why the strong law can have a deterministic limit at all (laws-of-large-numbers.md): the zero–one law tells us the limit is a constant before any computation identifies it as μ.
  • The radius of convergence of ∑k​Xk​zk is a.s. constant.
  • The second Borel–Cantelli lemma (independence.md §5) is the special case A={Ak​ i.o.} with the dichotomy resolved by the divergence criterion.

3. Hewitt–Savage

Independence plus identical distribution gives a stronger triviality, on a larger σ-algebra.

Definition. An event is exchangeable if it is invariant under every finite permutation of the coordinates. These form the exchangeable σ-algebra E⊇T.

Hewitt–Savage zero–one law. If X1​,X2​,… are i.i.d., every exchangeable event has probability 0 or 1.

Proof idea. Approximate an exchangeable A by an event An​ depending on the first n coordinates; apply the permutation swapping the first n with the next n, which preserves P (i.i.d.) and fixes A; conclude P(A∩πAn​)→P(A)2 and also →P(A). □

E is strictly larger than T: the event {Sn​=0 for some n} for a simple random walk is exchangeable but not tail (changing X1​ can change it). So Hewitt–Savage decides things Kolmogorov cannot — for instance that a simple random walk on Z is either recurrent or transient with probability one, never something in between.

The hypothesis is exactly identical distribution. For merely independent, non-identically-distributed sequences the exchangeable σ-algebra can be non-trivial. And if independence is dropped in favour of exchangeability alone, triviality fails completely: an exchangeable sequence is a mixture of i.i.d. ones, and the mixing parameter is an exchangeable random variable that is not constant — see exchangeability-de-finetti.md. The two theorems are the extreme and general cases of one picture.

A martingale-theoretic statement that contains the others, and explains them.

This is the martingale convergence theorem applied to the uniformly integrable martingale Mn​=E[1A​∣Fn​] (martingales.md). Read it as: accumulated information settles every question in the limit. Kolmogorov's law follows by taking A tail: then P(A∣Fn​)=P(A) for every n by independence, and the limit 1A​ must equal the constant P(A) a.s.

5. What These Results Do and Do Not Show

  • They are theorems about the measure, not predictions. "Either the series converges a.s. or it diverges a.s." does not tell you which; identifying the value requires the actual distribution (Kolmogorov's three-series theorem, for the convergence example).
  • The dichotomy is a consequence of independence, which is a modelling assumption (independence.md §6). Drop it and the dichotomy goes: the whole content of de Finetti's theorem is that exchangeable sequences have a genuinely random tail.
  • They explain why deterministic answers are available at all. Every limit statement in Part B — the strong law's limit, the CLT's normalizing constants, the law of the iterated logarithm's envelope — is a constant because a zero–one law says it must be. The subsequent work only computes which constant.

6. Where This Is Used

  • Laws of large numbers — the limit is constant before it is identified; the ergodicity of the i.i.d. shift is §2.
  • Independence §5 — Borel–Cantelli as the prototype.
  • Exchangeability and de Finetti — what replaces §3 when independence is dropped.
  • Martingales — §4, and the general machinery behind all of it.
