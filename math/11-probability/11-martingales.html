Martingales

A martingale is a process whose conditional expectation, given everything known so far, is its current value: a mathematical model of a fair game, and — more usefully — the general structure of "conditional expectation indexed by time".

Martingales are included here for three foundational reasons. They give a convergence theorem requiring neither independence nor identical distribution; they supply the machinery behind Lévy's zero–one law; and they are the setting in which sequential conditioning is unambiguous, the conditioning family being fixed in advance rather than chosen after the fact (conditional-expectation.md §6).

References: Williams, Probability with Martingales (the canonical treatment); Durrett, Probability, ch. 4; Kallenberg, Foundations, ch. 7.

1. Filtrations

Definition. A filtration is an increasing family F0​⊆>F1​⊆⋯⊆F of sub-σ-algebras. A process (Xn​) is adapted if Xn​ is Fn​-measurable for each n, and predictable if Xn​ is Fn−1​-measurable.

Fn​ is "what is known at time n" — the informational reading of a σ-algebra introduced in kolmogorov-axioms.md §2(b). The natural filtration of a process is Fn​=σ(X0​,…,Xn​). Predictability is the formal version of "decided before seeing the next outcome", and is exactly what a gambling strategy must satisfy.

Definition. An adapted, integrable process (Xn​) is a

  • martingale if E[Xn+1​∣Fn​]=Xn​ a.s.;
  • supermartingale if E[Xn+1​∣Fn​]≤Xn​ (decreasing on average — the unfavourable game);
  • submartingale if ≥.

By the tower property (conditional-expectation.md §3), E[Xn​∣Fm​]=Xm​ for all n≥m, and E[Xn​]=E[X0​] for every n: a martingale has constant mean.

Standard examples: sums Sn​=∑i≤n​Xi​ of independent centred variables; products of independent unit-mean positive variables; the Doob martingale Mn​=E[Y∣Fn​] for any fixed Y∈L1 (which by the tower property is automatically a martingale — the source of Lévy's law); and Sn2​−nσ2 for a centred random walk.

2. Stopping Times and Optional Stopping

Definition. A random time τ:Ω→{0,1,…}∪{∞} is a stopping time if {τ≤n}∈Fn​ for every n — whether to stop at n is decidable from information available at n.

"The first time the walk hits 10" is a stopping time; "the last time it visits 0" is not (it requires knowing the future). The distinction is the entire content of the next theorem.

Theorem (optional stopping). Let (Xn​) be a martingale and τ a stopping time. Then E[Xτ​]=E[X0​] provided any one of:

  1. τ is bounded;
  2. τ<∞ a.s. and (Xn∧τ​) is uniformly integrable;
  3. E[τ]<∞ and the increments are bounded.

Proof idea. (Xn∧τ​) is itself a martingale — this is the discrete stochastic integral ∑k​Hk​(Xk​−Xk−1​) with the predictable strategy Hk​=1{τ≥k}​ — so E[Xn∧τ​]=E[X0​] for every n, and the conditions license passing to the limit. □

"You cannot beat a fair game." The martingale property is preserved by every predictable strategy: ∑k​Hk​(Xk​−Xk−1​) has mean zero whenever H is predictable and suitably integrable. This is a theorem, not a slogan, and the hypotheses are where the classical "martingale" betting system lives: doubling after each loss gives τ<∞ a.s. and Xτ​=1 a.s., apparently contradicting E[Xτ​]=0. What fails is condition (2) — the stopped process is not uniformly integrable, since the required capital is unbounded. Unlimited credit, not clever betting, is what breaks the theorem.

3. Convergence

Doob's upcrossing inequality. If (Xn​) is a supermartingale and UN​[a,b] counts upcrossings of [a,b] up to time N, then (b−a)E[UN​[a,b]]≤E[(XN​−a)−].

Martingale convergence theorem. If (Xn​) is a supermartingale bounded in L1 (supn​E∣Xn​∣<∞), then Xn​→X∞​ almost surely with X∞​∈L1.

Proof. Failure of convergence means liminf<a<b<limsup for some rationals a<b, which forces infinitely many upcrossings; the inequality bounds their expected number, so each such event is null, and there are countably many pairs (a,b). □

The proof is a good example of the general strategy: a deterministic criterion for convergence (finitely many upcrossings of every rational interval), converted into an almost-sure statement by an expectation bound plus countability. No independence is used anywhere.

L1-boundedness does not give convergence in L1: the martingale Xn​=∏i≤n​(1+εi​) with i.i.d. ±1 signs εi​ has E[Xn​]=1 but Xn​→0 a.s. Mass escapes, exactly as in convergence-modes.md §3. The repair is uniform integrability:

Theorem (L1 convergence and closure). For a martingale (Xn​) the following are equivalent: (i) (Xn​) is UI; (ii) Xn​ converges a.s. and in L1; (iii) there is Y∈L1 with Xn​=E[Y∣Fn​] for all n (the martingale is closed). In that case X∞​=E[Y∣>F∞​].

Lévy's upward theorem. For Y∈L1 and Fn​↑>F∞​, E[Y∣Fn​]⟶E[Y∣F∞​]a.s. and in L1.

Taking Y=1A​ with A∈F∞​ gives Lévy's zero–one law §4. There is a matching downward theorem for decreasing filtrations, and it is the standard route to de Finetti's theorem (exchangeability-de-finetti.md) and to Etemadi-style proofs of the strong law.

Under Lp boundedness for p>1 everything is easier: Doob's maximal inequality ​supk≤n​∣Xk​∣​p​≤p−1p​∥Xn​∥p​ gives Lp boundedness of the maximum, hence UI, hence convergence in Lp.

4. Why Martingales Belong in a Foundations Folder

  • They generalize the limit theorems. Part B's results assume independence; the martingale convergence theorem assumes only a conditional-mean structure, and martingale versions of the CLT and the LLN follow. Independence is revealed as sufficient rather than necessary.
  • They make sequential conditioning well posed. The Borel–Kolmogorov ambiguity (conditional-expectation.md §6) arises when a null event is approached by an unspecified limiting procedure. A filtration specifies the procedure in advance, and Lévy's theorem then says the conditional probabilities converge to the truth. The lesson is that conditioning needs a conditioning structure, not that conditioning is defective.
  • They formalize "information". Adaptedness, predictability, and stopping times give precise content to knowing, deciding in advance, and observing — notions that the informal vocabulary of probability uses constantly and cannot otherwise define.
  • Optional stopping is a genuine impossibility theorem. It says no predictable strategy converts a fair game into a favourable one, with explicitly stated hypotheses that identify exactly which idealization ("unbounded capital") the familiar counterexamples exploit.

5. Where This Is Used

  • Zero–one laws §4 — Lévy's law.
  • Exchangeability and de Finetti — the downward theorem.
  • Brownian motion — the continuous-time analogues; Bt​ and Bt2​−t are martingales, and the Itô integral is the continuous stochastic integral of §2.
  • Conditional expectation — the tower property is the whole subject.
