Homomorphisms and the Isomorphism Theorems

A homomorphism is a map between groups that respects their operations. The homomorphisms out of a group are controlled by its normal subgroups, and the precise dictionary between the two is the content of the isomorphism theorems. This page builds on subgroups-cosets.md (normal subgroups and quotients) and supplies the structural language used everywhere downstream.

Homomorphisms

A homomorphism ϕ:G→H is a map preserving the group operation: ϕ(g1​g2​)=ϕ(g1​)ϕ(g2​). It automatically preserves the identity and inverses: ϕ(eG​)=eH​,ϕ(g−1)=ϕ(g)−1. A bijective homomorphism is an isomorphism, written G≅H; two isomorphic groups are "the same group with relabelled elements". Special cases:

  • Endomorphism — a homomorphism G→G.
  • Automorphism — an isomorphism G→G (see below).
  • Monomorphism / epimorphism — injective / surjective homomorphism.

Kernel and image

The kernel is the preimage of the identity: kerϕ≡ϕ−1(eH​)={g∈G:ϕ(g)=eH​}≤G, and the image is imϕ=ϕ(G)≤H.

  • kerϕ◃G is always normal (conjugating a kernel element stays in the kernel). Conversely every normal subgroup is a kernel — namely of the quotient map G→G/N, g↦gN.
  • ϕ is injective iff kerϕ={eG​} — only the identity maps to the identity.
  • imϕ is a subgroup of H, not necessarily normal.

Example: the determinant det:GL(n,R)→R× is a homomorphism with kernel SL(n,R). The covering map SL(2,C)→SO+(1,3) has kernel {±1} — see topology-covers.md.

The four isomorphism theorems

First (fundamental) isomorphism theorem. For any homomorphism ϕ:G→H, G/kerϕ≅imϕ.

Every homomorphism thus factors as a surjection onto a quotient followed by an injection: G↠G/kerϕ ∼ ​imϕ↪H. This is the organising principle — "images are quotients".

Second (diamond) isomorphism theorem. If H≤G and N◃G, then HN≤G, H∩N◃H, and H/(H∩N)≅HN/N.

Third isomorphism theorem. If N◃K◃G with N◃G, then K/N◃G/N and (G/N)/(K/N)≅G/K. "Quotients of quotients cancel."

Fourth (correspondence/lattice) theorem. For N◃G, the subgroups of G/N correspond bijectively and inclusion-preservingly to the subgroups of G containing N, matching normal to normal (stated already in subgroups-cosets.md).

These four are the workhorses behind the Sylow counting arguments and the composition-series machinery.

Automorphisms, the centre, and inner automorphisms

The automorphisms of G form a group Aut(G) under composition. Each g∈G gives an inner automorphism (conjugation) cg​:x↦gxg−1, and the map g↦cg​ is a homomorphism G→Aut(G) whose

  • kernel is the centre Z(G)={z:zg=gz ∀g}, and
  • image is the group of inner automorphisms Inn(G).

By the first isomorphism theorem, Inn(G)≅G/Z(G). The quotient Out(G)=Aut(G)/Inn(G) is the outer automorphism group. (Famously Out(Sn​) is trivial except for the exceptional Out(S6​)≅Z2​ — see permutation-groups.md.)

Inn(G)◃Aut(G) is normal, because conjugating an inner automorphism by any automorphism yields another inner one. Conjugation also drives the class equation and the whole action-theoretic viewpoint of group-actions.md.
