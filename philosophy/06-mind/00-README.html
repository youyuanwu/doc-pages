Philosophy of Mind

Reference notes on minds and their place in nature. A person sees a red surface, remembers a name, feels pain, calculates a sum, decides to speak, and reaches for a glass. These episodes are familiar from the inside and increasingly tractable from the outside, yet neither familiarity nor scientific detail settles what kind of phenomena they are. Are mental states identical with physical states, realised by them, emergent from them, or partly independent of them? How can a state of one thing be about another? Why is some information processing accompanied by experience? How can a thought cause an arm to move in a world whose physical causes already appear sufficient?

These pages treat philosophy of mind broadly. Consciousness is central, but it is not the whole subject: intentionality, perception, cognition, action, self-knowledge, emotion, and non-conscious mentality raise problems that cannot be reduced to the question of what experience feels like. The section also keeps intelligence, consciousness, agency, and personhood apart. A system may possess one without obviously possessing the others, and much of the contemporary debate about animals and machines turns on failing to say which capacity is at issue.

The treatment is philosophical rather than a course in psychology or neuroscience. Empirical findings constrain theories and sometimes overturn the examples on which they were built, but observations do not arrive labelled with their metaphysical meaning. Neural correlation is not identity, behavioural success is not by itself understanding, and a first-person report is neither incorrigible revelation nor mere noise. The method page develops the evidential roles of each without granting any one of them a monopoly.

Status. Complete as planned: the four-page spine and all thematic folders are written, published, and cross-linked.

Boundaries

Several neighbouring sections already own parts of the terrain:

  • The ontological question of which category persons and minds occupy begins in Persons and Minds in the Inventory, while the general machinery of grounding and levels remains in Ontology. This section develops what is distinctive about mental phenomena.
  • Numerical identity through time remains with Persistence. The future treatment of the self will concern subjectivity, unity, embodiment, and no-self views rather than duplicate the persistence debate.
  • Alternative possibilities, sourcehood, and moral responsibility remain in Free Will. Mental causation and the explanation of action belong here because they arise before the distinct question whether an action is free.
  • The use of consciousness in natural theology remains in Arguments from Consciousness and Reason. These pages examine the underlying philosophy of mind without treating theism or naturalism as the default conclusion.
  • Public linguistic meaning remains in Semantics and the Theory of Meaning. Mental content belongs here where the question is how thoughts represent at all.
  • The LLM Introspection series remains a worked case about machine self-report. This section supplies the general theories and evidential standards that case puts under pressure.

Contents

The spine

  • What Is Philosophy of Mind? - four master questions, six notions that must not be conflated, the recurring test cases, and the relation between philosophical argument and the sciences of mind.
  • The Mind-Body Problem - the inconsistent-looking package of mental reality, causal efficacy, physical completeness, and mental distinctness; interaction, reduction, supervenience, realisation, grounding, and emergence.
  • Positions on Mind and Body - a comparative map of dualism, identity theory, functionalism, non-reductive physicalism, eliminativism, panpsychism, and neutral monism, with the costs that distinguish them.
  • Method and Evidence in Philosophy of Mind - how first-person reports, behaviour, cognitive performance, neural intervention, dissociation, evolution, and thought experiments bear on theories without deciding them by themselves.

Mind and body

  • Substance Dualism - Descartes' modal and divisibility arguments; interaction, physical completeness, pairing, embodiment, hylomorphism, and the application to survival.
  • Property Dualism - irreducible phenomenal properties in physical organisms; conceivability, knowledge, psychophysical laws, causal exclusion, and epiphenomenalism.
  • Behaviourism - methodological, psychological, and logical versions; public criteria, multi-track dispositions, circularity, phenomenal experience, and the transition to functionalism.
  • The Mind-Brain Identity Theory - type and token identity, contingent scientific identities, bridge laws, multiple realisation, explanatory levels, and the causal advantage of identity.
  • Functionalism - machine-table, causal-role, Ramsey-Lewis, and psychofunctional versions; roles and realisers, liberalism, chauvinism, absent qualia, and inverted spectra.
  • Non-Reductive Physicalism and Emergence
    • supervenience, realisation, constitution, grounding, causal exclusion, intervention, proportionality, and weak versus strong emergence.
  • Eliminative Materialism - folk psychology as a theory, reduction versus elimination, self-refutation, predictive success, replacement vocabularies, and illusionism as a distinct revisionary target.

Consciousness

  • Knowledge Arguments - Mary and the replies from ability, acquaintance, new modes of presentation, phenomenal concepts, and indexical knowledge.
  • Conceivability, Zombies, and Modal Arguments

  • Representational Theories of Consciousness

  • Empirical Theories of Consciousness

  • Panpsychism, Russellian Monism, and Neutral Monism

Intentionality and mental content

Read the spine in order. The mind-body problem states the constraints before the positions relax or reinterpret them; otherwise the positions can look like a catalogue of labels. The method page then prevents a second mistake: treating evidence as theory-free, or treating philosophical underdetermination as permission to ignore evidence.

Scope

  • No definition of mind by consciousness alone. Belief, perception, intention, and unconscious cognition may be mental without being phenomenally conscious.
  • No definition of consciousness by report alone. Report is evidence generated by a mechanism; explaining that mechanism is not automatically explaining experience.
  • No inference from mechanism straight to metaphysics. A computational or neural model can explain a capacity while leaving open whether the realised state is identical with, grounded in, or merely correlated with its physical basis.
  • No automatic human exceptionalism. Human language gives unusually rich evidence, not a stipulated boundary around mentality.
  • No preferred theory by editorial fiat. Each view is presented through the phenomenon it explains best and the objection it finds hardest.

Prerequisites

No formal prerequisites are required. Readers unfamiliar with metaphysical dependence may first read Grounding and Fundamentality, Levels, and Priority. The philosophy-of-mind background in the LLM series is a shorter, application-oriented map; this section supersedes it as the general introduction while retaining it as a useful compact preview.
