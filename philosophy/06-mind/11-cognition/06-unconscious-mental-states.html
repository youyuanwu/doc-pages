Unconscious Mental States

Mental life extends beyond what is currently experienced or reportable. Standing beliefs persist while unattended; perception influences action without awareness; skills, memories, and biases shape behaviour without deliberate access. The category unconscious covers importantly different conditions, from dormant dispositions to active subpersonal processing and dynamically excluded motives.

The philosophical problem is not whether the brain processes information unconsciously. It plainly does. The question is when such processing constitutes a mental state of the person - a belief, perception, desire, or intention - rather than only a mechanism that supports one.

Three senses of unconscious

Dispositional unconscious. A standing belief that Paris is in France is not phenomenally present most of the day. It remains available for inference and report.

Cognitive unconscious. Active processes extract features, prime responses, guide movement, and update predictions without awareness.

Dynamic unconscious. In psychoanalytic traditions, motives and representations are kept from consciousness through conflict or defence while influencing thought and action.

These categories differ in evidence and mechanism. A dormant standing belief is not an active hidden sentence; unconscious perceptual processing is not automatically repressed; failure to report can reflect access limits rather than motivational exclusion.

Standing attitudes

Most beliefs and desires are standing rather than occurrent. A person believes their birth date while sleeping and desires a long-term goal while attending elsewhere.

Dispositional accounts identify these attitudes through tendencies to judge, infer, feel, and act under relevant conditions. The dispositions are structured by other attitudes and can be masked by distraction or conflicting goals.

Not every stored trace is a belief. A memory representation may influence performance without being available as something the person endorses. Standing belief requires an appropriate relation to reasoning, assertion, and revision, though the threshold is contested.

Subliminal and masked perception

Stimuli presented briefly or masked can influence choices and reaction times despite denied awareness. Such effects show unconscious processing of stimulus information. They do not automatically establish unconscious perception with rich content.

Methodological concerns include partial awareness, response bias, trial selection, and whether the awareness measure was sensitive to the feature driving behaviour. Objective discrimination and subjective report can diverge without either being a perfect measure of phenomenality.

Claims should match the evidence: feature sensitivity, semantic priming, or action guidance may be established at different depths. "The unconscious saw the word" can overstate a narrow effect.

Blindsight

Some people with damage to primary visual cortex deny seeing stimuli in part of the visual field while performing above chance when forced to guess. Blindsight separates visual information from ordinary visual experience and confident report.

The case is compared with neglect, agnosia, anosognosia, locked-in syndrome, and clinical disorders of consciousness in Disorders and Dissociations.

Interpretations differ:

  • genuine unconscious perception guides responses;
  • degraded conscious experience is present but falls below report criteria;
  • subpersonal visual processing supports guessing without personal-level perception.

The case does not decide among them without sensitive confidence, phenomenological, and mechanistic measures. It does establish that discrimination, access, and phenomenal experience should not be treated as one capacity.

Implicit memory and learning

Past experience can improve performance without conscious recollection. Priming, procedural learning, conditioning, and some amnesic capacities reveal memory systems that do not support episodic report.

Calling these implicit memory is appropriate when past experience causally changes a retained capacity. It does not imply an unconscious autobiographical memory with the same content as conscious recall.

The distinction between memory systems also pressures the idea of one mental store. A person's cognitive history is distributed across skills, dispositions, associations, semantic knowledge, and reconstructive episodes.

Implicit attitudes and bias

Indirect measures can reveal patterns in speed, association, attention, or behaviour that diverge from explicit avowals. These are often called implicit attitudes.

The label can imply more than the measure establishes. A task effect may reflect learned associations, familiarity, cultural knowledge, motor response, or context rather than a stable unconscious belief. Reliability and predictive reach vary.

The morally relevant point need not await a verdict about belief. Systematic biased behaviour and institutional effects matter even if the underlying state is a habit or association. Responsibility questions require evidence about control, awareness, and opportunities for correction, not one metaphysical label.

Psychoanalytic unconscious

Freudian theory attributes thoughts, wishes, and conflicts that are actively excluded from consciousness yet expressed through symptoms, dreams, slips, and behaviour. Unlike merely subpersonal processing, these states are person-level and often possess complex intentional content.

The explanatory style can be difficult to falsify when every denial counts as resistance and every outcome admits symbolic interpretation. Contemporary psychodynamic theories can be assessed through specific predictions about defence, attachment, affect regulation, and memory rather than broad retrospective narratives.

The philosophical contribution is the possibility that self-interpretation and agency are divided by motivated processes. The empirical status must be judged claim by claim.

Personal and subpersonal levels

A personal-level state belongs to the agent's network of reasons, perceptions, and actions. A subpersonal state belongs to mechanisms explaining how those capacities are implemented.

The distinction is explanatory, not a line between real and unreal. Edge detectors and prediction errors are real neural or computational states, but attributing a belief to them may be a category mistake. They do not individually endorse, infer, or act.

A subpersonal representation can have functional content without being a belief of the person. Personal attribution becomes warranted when the content is integrated with reasoning, flexible control, memory, and correction at the agent level.

Access and availability

Unconscious states vary in possible access:

  • permanently inaccessible processes;
  • states accessible with cues or attention;
  • information available to action but not report;
  • states available to one subsystem but not another;
  • contents that become conscious after processing.

This graded structure resists a binary conscious/unconscious inventory. It also connects with global-workspace theories, which define consciousness through broad availability.

Availability is not phenomenality by definition. A state may influence many systems without experience, and an experience may exceed what later report can access.

Are unconscious beliefs possible?

A person may behave as if they believe something they sincerely deny. Does this show an unconscious belief, a conflicting association, or a disposition without commitment?

Reasons to attribute belief include explanatory integration, stability, inferential effects, action guidance, and responsiveness to evidence. Reasons against include lack of endorsement, narrow context, and absence from reasoning.

Belief may be fragmented. A person can possess different dispositions in different contexts without one coherent hidden proposition. Treating belief as graded or context-sensitive may fit better than choosing between full belief and none.

The mark of the mental

If unconscious states are mental, consciousness cannot be the sole mark of mind. Intentionality, functional role, rational integration, and availability become alternative criteria.

Expanding mentality to every information-processing state creates the opposite problem. Immune systems, thermostats, and cells carry information and regulate action. A theory needs a principled level at which representation belongs to an agent rather than an observer's useful description.

The likely answer is plural and graded: basic subpersonal representation, personal attitudes, and conscious experience are related achievements, not interchangeable names.

Unconscious reasons

Can an agent act for a reason they do not consciously know? A belief and desire may guide action without occurrent awareness, provided the action is sensitive to their content and integrated with control. Conscious deliberation is not required for every reason-guided skill.

An unknown cause is not automatically a motivating reason. A priming effect can alter behaviour without being a consideration for which the person acted. Reasons explanations require content and an appropriate guiding role, as Reasons, Intentions, and Action argues.

This distinction prevents psychological causes from being redescribed as reasons merely because they influenced an action.

Unconscious cognition is not one hidden mind beneath consciousness. It is a collection of standing dispositions, active mechanisms, inaccessible representations, and sometimes person-level conflicts. Precise attribution requires matching the mental category to the evidence rather than treating every effect as an unconscious belief.
