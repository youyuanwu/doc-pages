The Problem of Mental Causation

Mental states appear to make things happen. Pain causes withdrawal, belief guides search, and intention produces movement. If these explanations are literal, mental properties are causally relevant to physical events. Yet physical science appears able to explain bodily motion through physical antecedents alone. The problem of mental causation is to reconcile mental efficacy with physical causal completeness without systematic overdetermination.

The problem is not one problem for every theory. Substance dualism must explain cross-domain interaction. Property dualism must explain what distinct mental properties add. Non-reductive physicalism must show that realised higher-level causes are not excluded by their physical bases. Content theories face a further issue: behaviour depends on what beliefs mean, while physical causation appears sensitive only to local physical structure.

Why mental causation matters

Without mental causation, ordinary explanations lose their apparent force:

  • she opened the window because she believed the room was hot;
  • he withdrew because the surface hurt;
  • they revised the proof because they noticed a contradiction;
  • the child reached because she wanted the toy.

These explanations do more than predict movement. They identify reasons, contents, and experiences under which action becomes intelligible. If the same bodily history would have occurred regardless of those mental properties, the explanations seem at best decorative.

Mental causation also supports knowledge. Reports about consciousness are evidence only if experiences or reliable representations help cause them. Reasoning is rational only if conclusions arise partly because of evidential and inferential relations, not merely because one neural event happens to follow another.

Physical causal completeness

The causal completeness of the physical is commonly stated:

Every physical event that has a cause at time t has a sufficient physical cause at t.

This is not the claim that every physical event is deterministic, that every cause is known, or that causal explanation must mention only microphysics. Probabilistic physical causes and higher-level physical causes can satisfy completeness. The thesis excludes a physical effect whose causal history requires an independent non-physical contribution.

Its support is methodological and historical. Physical inquiry has repeatedly replaced vital and mental interventions with continuous physical mechanisms, and conservation and dynamical laws do not display gaps awaiting non-physical causes. The principle is not an a priori truth, but denying it incurs an empirical burden.

The exclusion argument

Let mental property M be realised by physical property P1​, and let a later physical effect be P2​:

graph LR
  P1[Physical realiser P1] --> P2[Physical effect P2]
  P1 -. realises .-> M[Mental property M]
  M -. causes? .-> P2

The exclusion argument says:

  1. M causes P2​.
  2. P1​ is sufficient for P2​.
  3. M is distinct from P1​.
  4. P2​ is not systematically overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes.
  5. Therefore M cannot be an additional cause of P2​.

Every major response rejects or qualifies a premise. Identity theory denies 3. Interactionist dualism denies 2. Epiphenomenalism denies 1. Overdetermination views deny 4. Higher-level accounts deny that M and P1​ are independent competitors.

Overdetermination

An effect is overdetermined when two causes are each sufficient, as when two bullets strike a target simultaneously and either would have produced the result. Mental and physical causes could similarly overdetermine every action.

Systematic overdetermination is unattractive for three reasons. It would be an enormous coincidence that independent mental and physical causes always converge. It undermines ordinary counterfactual tests, since removing either cause leaves the effect. And it makes one causal route explanatorily dispensable.

The comparison may be unfair if mental and physical properties are not independent. Realisation binds them necessarily or lawfully; one cannot remove the mental cause while holding its physical realiser fixed. The event may have one causal history described at two levels rather than two bullet-like causes. Whether that dissolves exclusion depends on the theory of properties and causation.

Events and properties

A particular action can be one event under mental and physical descriptions. Davidson's token identity exploits this: the event described as a decision is identical with an event described neurally. There are not two events to compete.

The property exclusion problem remains. Did the event cause the movement in virtue of its mental property or only its physical property? If only physical properties enter laws and mechanisms, token identity may secure causal participation for the event while leaving mentality irrelevant.

This distinction separates two demands:

  • causal efficacy of mental events: mental events are causes;
  • causal relevance of mental properties: events cause outcomes because of their mental features.

A satisfactory account of reasons explanation usually needs the second. The fact that a belief means the route is blocked, not merely that some neural token occurs, explains why the subject turns.

The problem of content

Content externalism allows intrinsic duplicates to have thoughts with different wide contents. If their local physical effects are identical, wide content appears causally irrelevant to immediate behaviour.

One reply separates explanatory tasks. Narrow functional role causes and predicts local movement; wide content explains success, error, reference, and interaction over a broader environment. Another treats relational properties as causally relevant: a key opens a lock partly because it fits that lock, although fit is relational.

The issue is not unique to mind. Being poisonous, being currency, and being an allele are relational or higher-level properties that support causal explanation. The mental case is difficult because reasons appear to work through their semantic content, and a physical mechanism may be described without that content.

Causal relevance and difference-making

A difference-making account asks whether varying mental variable M under suitable conditions changes outcome B:

M⊥⊥B∣C.

The notation says M and B are not independent given background C. If changing a belief across its possible physical realisers systematically changes action, belief is a causal variable.

This supports multiple levels. Temperature causes pressure changes although each temperature is realised by molecular motion. Mental variables can similarly capture stable dependencies across heterogeneous neural states.

The challenge is intervention. One cannot change a realised belief while holding every physical fact fixed. Interventions must operate through a realiser, and critics argue that the physical change does all the work. Difference-makers answer that causes are variables in models, not occult additions to microphysical forces.

Mechanisms

A mechanistic account explains an effect through organised components and activities. Mental causation is legitimate when a mental state identifies an organisation that is implemented by, and helps locate, the mechanism producing action.

For example, believing that a target is left may be realised across a neural population whose output guides orienting. The belief-level description explains what information the mechanism uses; the neural account explains how it transforms signals. Neither alone answers every contrastive question.

Mechanistic integration is stronger than mere predictiveness. An attribution that compresses behaviour but maps onto no controllable organisation may be instrumentally useful without identifying a cause. Mental variables earn causal status through robust intervention, decomposition, and cross-context generalisation.

Powers and realization

On a powers account, causes produce effects through dispositions. A realised mental property may possess the same causal powers as a subset or organised profile of its realiser's powers. It adds no independent force and is not inert.

The subset proposal avoids overdetermination:

Powers(M)⊆Powers(P1​).

Mental properties remain distinct through their selected causal profile. Critics argue that this is reduction in all but name: if nothing belongs to M beyond physical powers, its autonomy is classificatory. Defenders reply that real higher-level properties need not add fundamental powers to support causation.

Downward causation

Downward causation is sometimes described as a higher-level mental state altering its physical components. If interpreted as an additional force, it conflicts with completeness. If interpreted as constraint, organisation, or control, it can be ordinary: system-level structure changes which component interactions occur without violating local laws.

A goal state may constrain motor processing; an organism's organisation channels molecular events. The causal work is realised through components, not imposed from outside physics. This modest downward causation fits non-reductive physicalism.

Strong emergence claims novel downward powers not exhausted by realisers. It faces the full exclusion problem and needs empirical laws specifying the additional influence.

Relation to free will

Mental causation is necessary for most accounts of agency but not sufficient for free will. A determined belief-desire process may genuinely cause an action while failing a libertarian condition; an indeterministic event may be free of determination while not controlled by reasons.

This folder asks how reasons and intentions produce action. The separate Free Will section asks whether that production provides alternative possibilities, sourcehood, and moral responsibility. Keeping the questions apart prevents causal efficacy from being mistaken for freedom.

Assessment

The exclusion problem is most forceful against properties claimed to be both distinct from the physical and independently efficacious. It is less forceful when mental and physical descriptions identify one realised process and answer different contrastive questions. The remaining issue is whether higher-level explanation tracks genuine difference-making or merely redescribes causes whose relevance is fixed entirely below.
