Pleasure and Desire

Pleasure and desire connect consciousness with value and action. Pleasure seems to make an experience good for its subject; desire presents an outcome as to-be-achieved and motivates pursuit. They often coincide and can separate. A person can want what brings no pleasure, enjoy what they do not want to continue, or pursue a long-term goal through unpleasant means.

Theories of pleasure ask whether diverse pleasant experiences share a sensation, a felt dimension, or an attitude of liking. Theories of desire treat it as a disposition to act, a representation with world-to-mind direction of fit, a reward signal, or a seeming that there is reason to pursue something. Dissociations between wanting and liking show why neither can simply define the other.

The heterogeneity of pleasure

The pleasures of taste, achievement, music, relief, exercise, and understanding have different sensory qualities. If pleasure is one feeling added to each, what common phenomenal element do they share?

Three options dominate:

  • distinctive-feeling theory: pleasure contributes a common positive sensation;
  • hedonic-tone theory: experiences vary along a positive-negative dimension without sharing one sensory quality;
  • attitudinal theory: pleasure consists in liking, favouring, or wanting an experience as it occurs.

The first struggles with introspective diversity. The second treats valence as a common dimension rather than a qualitative ingredient. The third explains diversity through a shared attitude but must say what liking is without circular appeal to pleasure.

Hedonic tone

On a dimensional view, experiences possess hedonic tone in degrees. Sweetness and warmth differ qualitatively while each can be positively valenced. Pleasure is not a separate object but the way an experience matters to the subject.

Valence can vary while sensory content remains stable. Adaptation, satiety, context, and learning can turn pleasant stimulation neutral or unpleasant. This supports a distinct affective dimension.

Dimensional models often add arousal and motivational direction. These spaces organise affect empirically but do not by themselves explain why positive valence feels good or grounds reasons.

Attitudinal theories of pleasure

An attitudinal theory says a subject takes pleasure in an experience when they desire, like, or favour it as it occurs. The object of pleasure can vary; the positive attitude supplies unity.

This handles cases where one person enjoys a sensation another dislikes. It also allows pleasure in complex activities without positing one common sensory quale.

Counterexamples involve unwanted pleasure and pleasure without reflective endorsement. A subject may enjoy a distraction they wish not to enjoy, or experience sensory pleasure before forming an attitude. The theory can use a lower-level liking rather than a deliberate desire, but then liking risks becoming another name for positive valence.

Pleasure and the good

Hedonism holds that pleasure is the only intrinsic good and pain the only intrinsic bad. Even without hedonism, pleasure normally provides a reason and contributes to welfare.

Experience-machine cases suggest people value achievement, relationship, knowledge, and reality beyond pleasurable experience. A hedonist may argue these are sources or contents of pleasure; critics say their value survives when pleasure is held fixed.

The philosophy of mind supplies the nature of pleasure; ethics asks how much value that nature carries. One should not infer hedonism merely from pleasure's positive valence.

Desire and direction of fit

Beliefs aim to fit the world; desires aim for the world to fit them. This world-to-mind direction of fit distinguishes wanting the door open from believing it open.

Desires have satisfaction conditions and can motivate action. A desire for water is satisfied when the relevant water-related condition obtains. It can persist without conscious feeling and compete with other desires.

Direction of fit identifies a normative role, not a mechanism. An intention also has world-to-mind fit but adds commitment and planning. A command has similar direction without being a desire of its hearer.

Dispositional theories of desire

Desire can be analysed as a disposition to act so as to bring about its object, given appropriate beliefs. To desire G is, roughly, to be disposed to choose means believed to promote G.

This connects desire with behaviour and decision. It faces familiar masking and circularity. Competing desires, weakness, uncertainty, and bodily limits can prevent action. Which behaviour a desire supports depends on beliefs and other desires.

A functional network can define attitudes together, but phenomenal wanting and normative reasons may not be exhausted by choice dispositions.

Reward and reinforcement

Reward-learning systems update action values from prediction error. A reward in this technical sense is a signal or objective shaping learning; it need not be consciously pleasurable. Likewise, a system can pursue a high-valued outcome without feeling desire.

Conflating reward, pleasure, and desire produces false conclusions about animals and machines. A reward function assigned during training is not automatically something the system wants, and neural reward prediction error is not identical with felt pleasure.

The concepts interact: reward learning can produce wanting, successful outcomes can produce pleasure, and pleasure can reinforce action. Their distinction is empirically demonstrated by dissociation.

Wanting and liking

Incentive-salience theories distinguish wanting - motivational pull and cue-triggered pursuit - from liking - hedonic impact. Addiction can increase wanting while pleasure declines. Conversely, a pleasant taste may be liked without motivating continued consumption after satiety.

This dissociation undermines theories that reduce desire to expected pleasure. It also explains compulsive pursuit that conflicts with reflective values.

Wanting itself has several levels: cue-driven incentive, occurrent felt urge, standing goal, and endorsed preference. A person can repudiate one while acting from it, as the free-will discussion of hierarchical desire illustrates.

Desire as a seeming of the good

Evaluative theories say desire presents its object as good or as providing a reason. This explains motivation through apparent value and connects desire with rational assessment.

The guise of the good thesis is too strong if agents knowingly desire what they regard as bad. A weaker view says desire creates a local attraction or reason-seeming without all-things-considered endorsement.

Desire can then be recalcitrant like emotion: the outcome continues to seem attractive despite judgment against it. This supports a perceptual analogy while preserving conflict among motivational systems.

Desire and reasons

Do desires create reasons or respond to independent value? Humean theories ground an agent's reasons in desires; anti-Humeans allow recognition of value to motivate without a prior desire.

The causal question is separate from the normative one. A desire can explain action without making it justified. The action chapter distinguishes motivating, explanatory, and normative reasons.

Preference formation also matters. Manipulated, adaptive, or compulsive desires may fail to express the agent's values even while strongly motivating.

Higher-order desire

Agents can desire not to be moved by a first-order desire. Frankfurt's willing and unwilling addicts illustrate this hierarchy. A second-order volition endorses which desire should become effective.

Hierarchy explains identification and self-governance but can regress: why identify with the higher desire rather than question it too? Wholeheartedness, history, and reasons- responsiveness are proposed stopping points.

The normative implications belong in Compatibilism. Here the hierarchy shows that "what the agent wants" can refer to conflicting levels rather than one scalar preference.

Pleasure, desire, and consciousness

Pleasure is ordinarily phenomenal; desire can be standing and unconscious. A system can represent goals and optimise rewards without any felt wanting or enjoyment. Conversely, a passive pleasure may occur without a plan to pursue it.

This distinction matters for moral status. Behavioural pursuit is evidence of preference and possible welfare, but not proof of valenced experience. Reports of enjoyment are evidence whose reliability depends on the reporting system.

Sentience concerns the capacity for positive and negative experience; agency concerns goal-directed control. They should not be inferred from one another.

Pleasure and desire are related but distinct dimensions of mind. Valence explains how a state is good or bad for the subject; desire explains motivational orientation; reward explains learning and control. Their dissociation is indispensable for theories of agency, addiction, welfare, and artificial systems.
