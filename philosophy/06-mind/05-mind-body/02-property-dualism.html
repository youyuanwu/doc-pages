Property Dualism

Property dualism holds that there is one kind of concrete substance - ordinarily the physical organism - but at least two irreducible kinds of property. Brains possess physical properties such as mass, structure, and electrical activity; minded organisms also possess phenomenal or other mental properties that are neither identical with nor reducible to physical properties. Unlike substance dualism, the view does not add a separable bearer. The person who feels pain is the embodied organism, not an immaterial object attached to it.

The position is attractive because it appears to respect both sides of the mind-body problem. Detailed brain dependence is expected, since mental properties are instantiated by physical systems under psychophysical laws. Phenomenal character remains irreducible, since no inventory of structural and causal facts seems to entail what an experience is like. The cost concentrates in causation: if irreducible mental properties do causal work, physical causal completeness is threatened; if they do not, our reports and knowledge of experience become puzzling.

The core thesis

Property dualism combines three claims:

  1. Substance monism: every concrete individual is physical; there are no immaterial minds in addition to organisms.
  2. Property irreducibility: at least some mental properties are not identical with, reducible to, or fully explicable in physical and functional terms.
  3. Lawful dependence: mental properties systematically depend on physical organisation, ordinarily through fundamental or emergent psychophysical laws.

The first claim distinguishes the view from substance dualism. The second distinguishes it from reductive physicalism. The third distinguishes it from a merely accidental parallelism between two sets of properties.

Most contemporary property dualism focuses on phenomenal properties: the felt character of seeing red, tasting coffee, or being in pain. A broader version also treats intentional or rational properties as irreducible, but content and rationality may have functional or externalist analyses unavailable for qualia. An argument that establishes irreducible experience should not quietly be extended to every mental kind.

Why properties rather than substances?

If brain injury removes a capacity, stimulation produces a sensation, and anaesthesia abolishes experience, a theory should make minds depend intimately on brains. Property dualism does so without identifying the dependent property with its basis. A fragile vase depends on its physical structure while fragility is described dispositionally; an economic recession depends on individual activity while remaining a macro-level property. The property dualist argues that consciousness is another dependent but real feature, with the crucial addition that it cannot be reduced even in principle.

This avoids the pairing problem for separate minds. One organism instantiates both physical and mental properties; no further fact pairs an unextended subject with a body. It also avoids asking how a disembodied substance stores memories or character. The organism supplies persistence and cognitive organisation.

The gain is limited. Saying that one bearer has both properties locates the correlation, but it does not explain why these physical properties yield these experiences. The psychophysical relation remains fundamental unless some account of grounding or emergence is added.

The conceivability route

The most influential argument begins from phenomenal zombies: beings physically and functionally identical to us but with no experience. If such beings are metaphysically possible, phenomenal facts are not necessitated by physical facts, and physicalism is false.

The argument has the form:

  1. A physical duplicate without phenomenal consciousness is ideally conceivable.
  2. Ideal conceivability is evidence of metaphysical possibility.
  3. If physical facts do not necessitate phenomenal facts, phenomenal properties are not physical properties.
  4. Therefore phenomenal properties are non-physical.

Property dualism then keeps the physical duplicate as a complete physical substance while adding a property absent in the zombie world. The controversial step is the move from conceivability to possibility. A physicalist may argue that phenomenal and physical concepts present one property in radically different ways, as pre-scientific and chemical concepts once presented water. The property dualist replies that a complete physical description specifies only structure and function, leaving out the subjective datum rather than merely redescribing it.

This exchange establishes at least an epistemic gap. Whether that gap is conceptual only or reflects distinct properties is the point at issue. The later consciousness pages will examine the modal argument directly; here its importance is that it supports property, not automatically substance, dualism.

The knowledge route

Frank Jackson's Mary knows every physical fact about colour vision while confined to a black-and-white environment. On first seeing red, she appears to learn what red looks like. The argument concludes that physical knowledge was incomplete because a phenomenal fact remained.

Property dualism reads the case straightforwardly: Mary acquires knowledge of an irreducible property. Physicalists offer several alternatives. She may acquire an ability to recognise and imagine red, acquaintance with an old fact, or a new phenomenal concept of a property already known physically. The dispute turns on whether new knowledge requires a new fact or merely a new epistemic relation to one fact.

The argument has an important limit. Even if Mary learns a non-physical fact, it does not show that phenomenal properties cause anything. It establishes an alleged ontological addition while leaving its integration with physical explanation open.

Naturalistic and emergent dualism

Property dualism need not invoke supernatural substances. Naturalistic dualism treats phenomenal properties and psychophysical laws as fundamental features of nature, comparable in status to basic physical properties and laws. A completed science would therefore contain both physical dynamics and laws connecting physical organisation to experience.

Emergent dualism holds that sufficiently organised matter instantiates genuinely novel properties, perhaps with novel causal powers. The dependence is asymmetric: no brain, no human consciousness, but consciousness is not reducible to the brain's microphysical properties. This is strong emergence, not merely the weak emergence of a pattern difficult to predict or simulate.

Both positions face a question of modal strength. If the same physical organisation could have different phenomenal properties under different laws, the connection is nomological rather than metaphysically necessary. If physical organisation necessarily grounds the phenomenal, the view approaches non-reductive physicalism. The labels matter less than the claimed dependence relation.

Psychophysical laws

A fundamental law might map a physical state P to a phenomenal property Q:

P⇒L​Q.

The arrow expresses lawful necessitation, not logical derivation or identity. Such laws could explain why brain duplicates share experiences in the actual world even if zombies remain metaphysically possible.

Three questions follow.

  • Why this mapping? Why does one neural organisation yield red rather than green, or experience rather than none? A fundamental law may have no deeper explanation, but the unexplained mapping is exactly what motivated the view.
  • How fine-grained is it? A useful law must connect physical organisation to specific phenomenal character, not merely state that complex brains are conscious.
  • How is it tested? Experience is measured through report and behaviour, which are physical outputs. If phenomenal properties do not affect those outputs, evidence for the mapping becomes indirect in a particularly severe way.

Treating the laws as fundamental is not incoherent. Fundamental physics also contains brute constants and relations. The comparative question is whether psychophysical laws earn their cost through prediction and unification rather than restating every observed correlation separately.

The causal exclusion problem

Suppose pain property M is instantiated because of physical base P1​, and a bodily movement P2​ follows. If P1​ is already sufficient for P2​, what causal work does M perform?

  P1 --> M[Phenomenal pain M]
  M -. disputed .-> P2

Four responses are available:

  1. Deny completeness: M supplies causal influence not contained in P1​.
  2. Accept overdetermination: P1​ and M are both sufficient causes.
  3. Accept epiphenomenalism: M is caused but does not cause physical events.
  4. Reject competition: M is a higher-level cause realised by P1​, so the arrows describe one causal process at different levels.

The fourth response is natural for non-reductive physicalism but unstable for robust property dualism. If M is a distinct property with independent causal power, the competition returns; if its efficacy is nothing over the realiser's efficacy, its ontological distinctness appears causally idle. The dispute is not whether explanations can occur at several levels, but whether irreducible properties contribute distinct powers.

Epiphenomenalism

An epiphenomenalist accepts physical-to-mental causation and denies mental-to-physical causation. Neural activity produces pain; pain itself produces no movement. The view preserves physical completeness and phenomenal irreducibility cleanly.

Its consequences are difficult:

  • Reports of pain are physically caused without the pain helping to cause them.
  • Memories and beliefs about experience are formed without phenomenal properties affecting memory or belief.
  • Natural selection cannot favour phenomenal properties through their effects, since they have none.
  • Our apparent knowledge of qualia cannot rely on ordinary causal contact with them.

The epiphenomenalist can answer that physical and phenomenal properties are linked by laws, so the physical process that causes a report also reliably gives rise to the reported experience. Correlation need not be accidental. But the semantic fit between report and experience remains striking: why does the physically produced sentence "this hurts" track the causally inert hurt rather than some unrelated property?

The causal argument for physicalism

The exclusion problem can be turned into a compact argument:

  1. Mental events cause physical events.
  2. Every caused physical event has a sufficient physical cause.
  3. The effects are not systematically overdetermined by independent causes.
  4. Therefore mental causes are physical causes, or their efficacy is wholly realised by physical causes.

A property dualist must reject or reinterpret at least one premise. Interactionists reject the second; epiphenomenalists reject the first for irreducible phenomenal properties; overdetermination views reject the third; non-competing-cause views deny that the conclusion yields reduction.

The argument's force depends on the type of mental property. A content property may be causally explanatory at a proportional higher level even when physically realised. An irreducible phenomenal property whose nature makes no difference to physical dynamics is harder to place. Property dualism is therefore most secure about irreducibility precisely where it is least secure about causation.

Does the view explain consciousness?

Property dualism is often motivated by the failure of physical explanation. It does not automatically supply a positive explanation. Declaring phenomenal properties fundamental explains why reduction fails, much as declaring charge fundamental explains why it is not reduced to another physical property. Yet charge is embedded in laws that predict and unify observable interactions. A phenomenal primitive needs comparable work if the view is to be more than a stopping point.

The property dualist may respond that not every true explanation reduces its target. Fundamental laws are legitimate explanations, and demanding a non-phenomenal account of phenomenality may be a demand to derive one basic kind from another. This response is strongest if independent reasons support the laws or if rival theories demonstrably leave the phenomenon out rather than merely leave it unexplained for now.

Assessment

CriterionVerdict
Phenomenal characterPreserved - irreducibility is the view's central motivation.
Brain dependenceStrong fit - one physical bearer instantiates mental properties under laws.
Mental causationWeak point - interaction threatens completeness; epiphenomenalism threatens report and knowledge.
Explanatory gainContested - fundamental laws may explain or merely codify correlations.
ParsimonyBetter than substance dualism - no second bearer, but additional properties and laws remain.
Empirical discriminationDifficult - physical duplicates behave alike whether or not phenomenal properties add causal work.

Property dualism isolates the strongest anti-reductive intuition while accepting the organism studied by science as the subject. Its central dilemma is correspondingly sharp: make phenomenal properties causally potent and explain their place in physical dynamics, or make them causally inert and explain how thought, report, and knowledge track them.
