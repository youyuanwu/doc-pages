Illusionism and Mysterianism

Illusionism and mysterianism agree that the ordinary demand for an explanation of consciousness cannot be met in the expected way. They disagree about why. The illusionist argues that introspection misdescribes complex representational and cognitive processes as possessing intrinsic phenomenal properties; explain the misdescription and no further property remains. The mysterian argues that consciousness is real but its physical basis may lie beyond human cognitive capacities.

One position relocates the explanandum from qualia to our judgments about qualia. The other retains the explanandum and limits the possible explainer. Both are easily caricatured as surrender. Their serious forms make testable or at least assessable claims about introspection and cognitive limits.

What illusionism denies

Illusionism does not deny that people are awake, perceive, discriminate, suffer, attend, or report rich experience. It denies phenomenal realism as traditionally conceived: the claim that experience instantiates intrinsic, ineffable qualitative properties presented directly and infallibly to the subject.

The word "illusion" is potentially misleading. A visual illusion still has an appearance, so critics ask whether phenomenal illusion already presupposes phenomenality. Keith Frankish therefore distinguishes the problematic properties from the real representational, cognitive, and reactive states that lead subjects to judge they have them. The illusion is not nothing; it is a systematic misrepresentation of those states.

The view is continuous with the revisionary strand of eliminative materialism, but it need not eliminate beliefs or every use of "conscious." Its target is a specific conception of qualia.

Heterophenomenology

Dennett's heterophenomenology treats subjects' reports as data about how things seem to them without granting that every apparent property exists as described. The method constructs a neutral catalogue of judgments, memories, reactions, and discriminations, then explains their production through cognitive science.

This respects first-person testimony without making it incorrigible. If subjects report a detailed, stable visual field while experiments reveal sparse access and change blindness, a theory should explain both the confidence and the limitations.

Critics argue that neutralising reports changes the subject. A complete explanation of why someone says "red has this quality" may leave the quality itself untouched. The illusionist answers that the alleged leftover has no independent identity conditions; every attempt to specify it returns to discrimination, memory, reaction, or further judgment.

The meta-problem strategy

The meta-problem asks why people judge that consciousness poses a hard problem. Candidate mechanisms include:

  • introspective models that represent outcomes while omitting construction;
  • attention that makes contents available but hides processing;
  • direct recognitional concepts with no inferential bridge to physical concepts;
  • social and linguistic practices that reify a private inner object;
  • cognitive architectures that cannot represent their own causal basis in detail.

Explaining these could debunk some anti-physicalist intuitions. A genuine debunking argument must show that the mechanisms generate the judgment whether or not qualia exist. A causal explanation alone is neutral: vision also has a causal explanation, but that does not show there are no visible objects.

The hard question for illusionism

If illusionism is true, why does the alleged illusion itself seem phenomenal? Saying that a system represents itself as phenomenally conscious appears to leave open whether the representation is consciously experienced. Adding another representation threatens a regress.

Illusionists reject the premise that seeming requires an intrinsic phenomenal item. "It seems to me that p" can be analysed as being disposed to judge, report, remember, and act as though p, under the control of a self-model. No extra glow accompanies the representation.

This is the decisive disagreement with phenomenal realism. The debate cannot be settled by agreeing that reports have mechanisms. It turns on whether the functional and representational account captures the minimal what-it-is-like datum or explains only why subjects assert it.

Ethical caution

Illusionism is not the claim that pain does not matter. Even if pain lacks intrinsic qualia as traditionally conceived, aversive states can dominate attention, drive avoidance, disrupt agency, and ground interests. An account that revises phenomenal ontology need not revise every moral conclusion.

The distinction is especially important for non-human systems. Denying robust qualia does not make behavioural and functional evidence irrelevant; it changes which properties underwrite welfare. Conversely, a theory should not use the word "illusion" to dismiss reported suffering while retaining the mechanisms that make suffering practically destructive.

Mysterianism

New mysterianism, associated with Colin McGinn, holds that consciousness has a natural physical explanation but human minds are cognitively closed to the concepts needed to grasp it. Just as some animals cannot understand arithmetic, evolved human cognition may be unable to represent the property linking brain and experience.

The position is not dualism. The missing relation may be wholly natural. Nor is it the modest claim that current science lacks an answer. It proposes a principled mismatch between our cognitive capacities and the explanatory task.

McGinn argues that introspection presents consciousness without its neural relation, while perception presents the brain without the property that yields consciousness. No available faculty combines the two modes. The closure is architectural, not merely a shortage of data.

Three kinds of mystery

Claims of mystery should be separated:

  1. Current ignorance: no adequate theory exists yet.
  2. Human cognitive closure: an answer exists but humans cannot understand it.
  3. Incoherent demand: the apparent question results from a conceptual mistake and has no answer of the requested kind.

Scientific optimism rejects the move from 1 to 2. Mysterianism affirms 2. Illusionism often approaches 3 for the traditional hard problem while preserving empirical questions about access and self-representation.

Evidence for closure is difficult. Persistent failure may reflect difficulty rather than impossibility, and claiming to know enough about the missing explanation to prove it unknowable looks unstable. Analogies with animal limits show that closure is possible, not that this problem exceeds us.

Nagel without permanent mystery

Nagel's argument about perspective is sometimes read as mysterian. His conclusion is more cautious: current objective concepts do not capture subjective character, and a future objective phenomenology may partly bridge the gap. The detailed bat essay explicitly leaves physicalism open.

Mysterianism turns this present conceptual limitation into a permanent species-level boundary. That requires an account of why conceptual innovation, instruments, mathematics, and artificial assistance cannot extend the relevant capacities.

Opposite diagnoses

Is there a complete natural explanation?Yes, through cognitive and representational mechanisms.Yes, but humans may be unable to grasp it.

Main riskExplaining judgments while leaving experience untouched.Turning present ignorance into an unfalsifiable permanent limit.

The positions mark opposite limits of optimism. Illusionism expects enough mechanism to dissolve the problem; mysterianism expects no human mechanism of inquiry to solve it. Both must explain why their diagnosis is better than the ordinary possibility that the problem is genuine, difficult, and tractable only after conceptual and empirical change.
