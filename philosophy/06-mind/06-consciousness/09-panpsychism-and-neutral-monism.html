Panpsychism, Russellian Monism, and Neutral Monism

Panpsychism and neutral monism reject the attempt to derive consciousness from a base described as wholly non-experiential. Panpsychism assigns experiential or proto- experiential features to fundamental physical entities. Russellian monism argues that physics describes causal structure while leaving the intrinsic nature of its bearers open, and proposes that this nature grounds consciousness. Neutral monism treats mental and physical facts as organisations or aspects of a basis that is itself neither.

These views promise continuity rather than emergence from nothing. Their central cost is the combination problem: explaining how micro-level features yield one unified subject with a structured experience.

The constitutive argument

A common motivation is:

  1. Consciousness is real.
  2. Consciousness cannot be reductively explained through structure and function alone.
  3. Radical emergence of consciousness from wholly non-experiential matter is unintelligible or requires brute laws.
  4. Therefore the fundamental base must already contain experiential, proto-experiential, or neutral features capable of constituting consciousness.

The argument does not infer that electrons think or possess human-like points of view. The fundamental feature may be extremely simple and lack cognition, agency, memory, or sensory content. The proposal concerns the ingredients of phenomenality, not miniature persons.

The third premise carries the weight. A critic may accept brute psychophysical laws, functional reduction, or strong emergence rather than populate the base with unfamiliar properties. Panpsychism exchanges the emergence problem for combination.

Constitutive panpsychism

Constitutive panpsychism holds that the consciousness of a complex subject is constituted by experiential properties of its fundamental parts. It is attractive to those who accept causal closure and reject epiphenomenal properties: if experiential features are intrinsic aspects of physical entities, mental causation is physical causation under its intrinsic description.

The view is not equivalent to animism. It need not attribute life, purpose, intelligence, or agency to ordinary objects. A rock contains fundamental entities with the relevant features but may fail to organise them into a unified macro-subject.

That qualification leads immediately to the question of why brains compose subjects while rocks do not. Functional or integrative organisation may be required, but then the theory must explain how organisation transforms many subjects or features into one.

Panprotopsychism

Panprotopsychism assigns fundamental entities properties that are not themselves phenomenal but can constitute phenomenality in combination. This avoids ubiquitous micro-experience while retaining a special basis for consciousness.

The difficulty is specifying the proto-property. If it is defined only as whatever produces consciousness, the account renames the unknown ground. If it is structurally or dispositionally described, the original hard problem returns: why does that structure yield experience?

The view gains content when proto-properties have independently motivated roles or laws. Without them it occupies an unstable midpoint between physicalist emergence and panpsychism.

Russellian monism

Russell and Eddington observed that physics characterises matter through mathematical structure, causal relations, and dispositions. It tells us how entities behave and relate, not necessarily what bears those relations intrinsically. Russellian monism places phenomenal or proto-phenomenal nature in this alleged categorical gap.

The proposal has three attractions:

  • it preserves the structural truths of physics;
  • it gives consciousness a place in the physical base rather than adding an interacting realm;
  • it explains why objective structural knowledge does not reveal phenomenal character: structure omits intrinsic nature by design.

The first objection disputes the gap. Physical properties may be exhausted by their causal-dispositional roles, leaving no hidden categorical nature. The second asks why the categorical base has the particular phenomenal structure humans experience. The third is epistemic: if intrinsic natures are known only through one's own consciousness, claims about their distribution elsewhere become difficult to test.

Neutral monism

Neutral monism holds that reality's basic elements are neither mental nor physical as ordinarily understood. Mental and physical domains arise from different relations, organisations, or perspectives on the neutral base. William James's "pure experience" and Russell's event-like constructions are historically important versions.

Unlike panpsychism, neutral monism need not call the basic elements experiential. Unlike physicalism, it denies that the physical description is metaphysically complete. The mind-body relation becomes a relation between two organisations of one basis rather than interaction between independent kinds.

The word "neutral" can conceal rather than solve the problem. A useful account must say what the base is, how mental and physical structures are constructed, and why only some organisations support unified consciousness. If the base is characterised only by its ability to become either, neutrality is promissory.

The combination problem

Suppose fundamental entities have experiences. Why should their arrangement produce one further subject rather than a collection? Summing colours, intensities, or information does not obviously sum points of view.

The problem has several forms:

  • Subject-summing: how do many subjects constitute one numerically distinct subject?
  • Quality combination: how do simple qualities constitute the qualities of complex experience?
  • Structural mismatch: micro-experiences lack the spatial, temporal, and intentional structure of human consciousness.
  • Boundary: why does one brain compose one subject while larger or overlapping systems do not?

Merely invoking integration states a condition without explaining constitution. If the combining relation is itself fundamental, panpsychism adds a psychophysical primitive comparable to the emergence law it sought to avoid.

Responses to combination

Phenomenal bonding posits a relation capable of unifying micro-subjects. It directly answers the demand but must explain the bond and its distribution.

Cosmopsychism reverses direction: the cosmos is the fundamental subject, and local subjects derive through decomposition or restriction. This avoids subject-summing and inherits a decombination problem: how one cosmic perspective yields many private, bounded perspectives.

Priority views treat consciousness at one level as fundamental rather than deriving it from smaller subjects. They align with priority monism in Fundamentality, Levels, and Priority but risk losing panpsychism's promised bottom-up continuity.

Non-subject panpsychism assigns phenomenal qualities without subjects at the base. It softens summing but must explain how subjectivity emerges from subjectless qualities.

Causation and empirical content

If intrinsic phenomenal nature realises the causal roles physics describes, it need not add forces or violate completeness. This is an advantage over interactionist dualism. But causal compatibility can make the theory empirically indistinguishable from physicalism: every observable effect follows the same structural laws whatever the intrinsic nature.

The theory may still be supported abductively if it explains consciousness with fewer brute connections. The comparison must count all primitives. Ubiquitous intrinsic properties plus a combination relation may not be simpler than physically grounded consciousness or fundamental psychophysical laws.

Claims about which systems form subjects can generate empirical consequences when tied to integration or organisation. Those consequences derive from the added principle, not from panpsychism alone.

Panpsychism and physicalism

Whether panpsychism counts as physicalism depends on definition. If the intrinsic features are the categorical nature of physical properties, the view may call itself physicalist. If physicalism means that structural-dynamical physical truths necessitate all truths, panpsychism rejects it.

The terminological issue should not hide the substantive claims:

  1. phenomenal or proto-phenomenal nature is fundamental;
  2. physics as ordinarily formulated does not fully describe that nature;
  3. macro-consciousness depends on a constitutive relation not supplied by standard physical structure alone.

These claims distinguish the view regardless of label.

Assessment

These positions take the explanatory gap seriously without adding a Cartesian substance. They offer a principled reason objective physics might omit phenomenal nature while remaining empirically successful. Their promise is continuity; their liability is that moving consciousness into the base does not yet explain the structured, bounded subjects actually encountered.

The decisive comparison is between two brutenesses: emergence of experience from a non-experiential base and combination or decomposition within an experiential or neutral base. Neither is removed by changing the level at which consciousness is declared fundamental.
