Consciousness and Content

Intentionality and consciousness are conceptually distinct: a state can represent without being conscious, and an experience can seem objectless. The deeper question is whether they are explanatorily independent. Phenomenal intentionality theories hold that the original or fundamental aboutness of mental states is grounded in phenomenal character. Separatist theories explain content through causal, biological, functional, and social relations that do not require experience.

The dispute matters because naturalistic theories readily assign content to unconscious and artificial systems. If consciousness is the source of original content, those assignments may capture only derived or observer-relative representation. If content is independent, demonstrating meaning says little about whether there is anything it is like to be the system.

The independence case

Several cases motivate separation.

  • Early visual and motor systems represent features outside awareness.
  • Blindsight supports discrimination and action without ordinary visual experience.
  • Standing beliefs retain content while not occurrently conscious.
  • Public inscriptions and computational states can have correctness conditions without plausible phenomenal character.
  • A diffuse mood may be phenomenally vivid while lacking a determinate object.

These suggest two axes rather than one:

Consciousseeing a red apple; occurrent thoughtdiffuse mood; perhaps raw pain

Each cell is contested, but the taxonomy prevents an argument for one property from being presented as an argument for the other.

Phenomenal intentionality

Phenomenal-intentionality theorists argue that some experiences possess content in virtue of what they are like. A visual experience presents red and round because its phenomenal character has that directed structure; an occurrent thought's cognitive phenomenology presents a proposition as understood rather than as an uninterpreted string.

The thesis can be modest or strong:

  • Modest: some conscious contents are partly fixed by phenomenal character.
  • Grounding: all original mental content is grounded in phenomenal intentionality, while unconscious states have content through relations to conscious ones.
  • Identity: phenomenal character itself is a kind of intentional content.

The modest thesis is compatible with many naturalistic accounts. The grounding thesis challenges them by making consciousness explanatorily prior.

Why invoke phenomenality?

Causal and functional theories face indeterminacy. One neural state covaries with many conditions and participates in many roles. The way an experience presents its object appears more determinate from the subject's perspective.

Phenomenality also promises original meaning. A picture or word means something because users interpret it. Conscious thought seems to present its content without a further interpreter, stopping the regress. The subject does not inspect an internal symbol and assign meaning; the thought is already experienced as about Paris or as the thought that snow is white.

Critics question both appearances. Introspection may not reveal content with the determinacy assumed, and conscious thought may inherit meaning from learning, external history, and inferential role. Feeling understood is not by itself what makes a thought true or false of the world.

Cognitive phenomenology

Does thinking have a phenomenal character beyond imagery, inner speech, and emotion? Consider hearing a sentence in a language one understands and then in one one does not. The acoustic experience may be similar while the understood sentence seems different. Supporters call this cognitive phenomenology: a distinctive what-it-is-like of grasping meaning.

Other proposed examples include suddenly understanding a proof, entertaining versus judging a proposition, and the tip-of-the-tongue state. Opponents explain the differences through associated imagery, affect, attention, action-readiness, and dispositional access rather than proprietary cognitive qualia.

Even if cognitive phenomenology exists, it may reveal the subject's mode of access rather than fix wide content. Earth and Twin Earth subjects could feel phenomenally alike while thinking about different substances. Phenomenal character may determine a narrow presentation while environment fixes reference.

Unconscious content

The grounding view must explain unconscious representation. One proposal gives an unconscious state content through its disposition to become conscious. Another grounds it in causal relations to conscious states within the same system. A standing belief means what it does because it can generate conscious judgments, imagery, and action.

This is strained for permanently unconscious subpersonal representations, early vision, and simple animals whose conscious conceptual capacities are uncertain. If those states guide behaviour with stable correctness conditions, making their content depend on a possible conscious interpretation may be unnecessary.

Phenomenal-intentionality theorists can distinguish basic content from derivative subpersonal content. The cost is a hierarchical theory that must specify the derivation without treating unconscious mechanisms as meaningless until noticed.

Objectless experience

Moods, bodily feelings, and some meditative states are candidates for phenomenal states without intentional objects. A separatist takes them as evidence that phenomenality is not essentially representational.

Intentionalists respond that moods represent the world globally as threatening, welcoming, empty, or significant; bodily feelings represent internal condition; even apparently contentless states present a temporal and bodily field. The content need not be propositionally articulated.

The debate risks triviality if any phenomenal difference is redescribed as representing that very difference. An intentional analysis should provide independent accuracy conditions and explanatory consequences.

Same content, different character

Seeing, imagining, remembering, and desiring can concern the same object while feeling different. This suggests that content does not fix phenomenality. Representationalists appeal to different modes of representing; phenomenal-intentionality theorists may say mode itself has intentional structure.

Inverted-spectrum cases push further by holding functional and representational content fixed while varying quality. If possible, phenomenality and content are independent. If complete content includes mode and every discriminatory relation, the inversion may be underspecified.

The right conclusion may be asymmetric: content does not exhaust phenomenality, while phenomenality partly fixes a narrow kind of content.

Externalism and phenomenal content

Externalism allows internal and phenomenal duplicates to differ in wide content. This conflicts with a strong claim that phenomenal character fixes all content. Several responses are available:

  1. reject externalism and make content internally phenomenal;
  2. distinguish narrow phenomenal content from wide referential content;
  3. hold that environment partly constitutes phenomenal character;
  4. accept that phenomenality grounds only some original content.

The second is the most common compromise. It preserves the first-person presentation while allowing reference to depend on the world and community. It also concedes that phenomenality alone does not determine everything thought.

The separatist program

Separatists use naturalistic content, functional role, and external relations to explain representation independently. Consciousness then affects access, salience, mode, or subjective character rather than basic correctness conditions.

This program explains unconscious content and makes machine representation possible in principle. Its pressure point is meaning for the system. A state can be interpreted by designers or observers as carrying information, but what makes the content intrinsic to the system rather than assigned from outside?

Teleological consumer roles and autonomous error correction answer by locating standards within the system's history and control organisation. Phenomenalists reply that these are still external descriptions of function and never produce understanding.

The LLM test case

Language models make the separation vivid. Their outputs can have public sentence meaning, participate in inference, and be corrected by tools and users. Those facts support content on externalist, use, and functional accounts. They do not establish phenomenal consciousness, because the very theories granting content were designed to make it independent of private experience.

The LLM introspection case study develops this point in detail. The philosophical lesson generalises: semantic competence can narrow the problem of machine mind without resolving whether a subject experiences the competence.

No simple entailment runs from consciousness to content or content to consciousness. The live issue is explanatory priority: whether phenomenal presentation supplies the basic norm of aboutness, or whether biological and functional representation explains content before consciousness enters. The next distinction - original versus derived intentionality - states that conflict in its sharpest form.
