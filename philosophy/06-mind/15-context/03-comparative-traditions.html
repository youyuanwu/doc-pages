Comparative Traditions on Mind and Self

Indian, Buddhist, and Chinese traditions developed sustained arguments about consciousness, self, perception, and cognition outside the substance-dualism framework that organises much modern European philosophy. They should not be translated too quickly into "physicalism" and "dualism." Their categories answer different questions: what bears consciousness, whether awareness reveals itself, how persons persist, what causes suffering, and whether heart and mind form one faculty.

This page compares arguments rather than presenting traditions as homogeneous. Nyaya, Samkhya, Advaita, Buddhist schools, and Chinese accounts disagree internally and with one another. The aim is to widen the decision space of the section, not assign each a Western label.

Nyaya: a substantial self

Nyaya defends an enduring atman distinct from body and momentary mental qualities. Cognition, desire, pleasure, pain, and effort are properties of this self.

One argument begins from memory and recognition: "I who saw this now remember it." Memory connects past and present cognition through one subject. A mere sequence of momentary states appears unable to own both experiences and recognise their identity.

Nyaya also appeals to coordinated desire and action across sensory modalities. The one who sees an object can later reach for it and remember its taste. A unified bearer explains cross-modal integration.

Buddhist opponents answer with causal continuity. A later state can inherit information from an earlier one without one permanent substance, as one flame causes another. Nyaya argues that causal connection alone cannot yield first-person ownership or genuine memory rather than a memory-like representation.

The dispute closely resembles the choice between a simple subject and an organised bundle in What Is the Self?, but Nyaya's epistemology and metaphysics should not be collapsed into Cartesian substance dualism.

Nyaya perception and cognition

Nyaya develops a realist epistemology of pramanas, reliable means of knowledge, including perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Perception arises through appropriate contact among self, mind, sense faculty, and object.

This layered account distinguishes the enduring subject from the internal organ (manas) coordinating episodic cognition. The self is not continuously manifest in consciousness; cognition is an adventitious property.

The view makes knowledge world-involving and fallible while retaining a substantial knower. Its complexity reflects a problem shared with contemporary theory: one must connect object, sensory mechanism, attention, cognition, and subject without treating them as one event.

Samkhya: consciousness and nature

Samkhya distinguishes purusha, pure consciousness, from prakriti, nature. Mind, intellect, ego, sensation, and material processes belong to prakriti; purusha is the witness for whom these processes appear.

The view is dualist, but not Cartesian in the ordinary sense. The mental machinery of thought and personality is on the natural side; pure consciousness is inactive and plural across subjects. Bondage arises when consciousness identifies with processes of nature, and liberation through discriminating their difference.

The causal relation is difficult. If purusha is inactive, how does its presence matter, and how are individual witnesses paired with streams of prakriti? The problem resembles epiphenomenal and pairing objections while arising from different soteriological aims.

Advaita Vedanta: consciousness as non-dual

Advaita identifies the deepest self, Atman, with Brahman, non-dual reality. Ordinary individuality, subject-object distinction, and plurality belong to ignorance or conventional appearance rather than ultimate truth.

Consciousness is not a property possessed by one object among others. It is the self-revealing condition under which objects and mental states appear. Attempts to know it as an object miss its status as the witness.

Advaita therefore differs from both substance dualism and panpsychism. It does not add many private conscious substances to a physical world; it challenges the ultimate reality of the division among subjects and objects.

The central objections concern individuation and illusion. If one consciousness is ultimate, what explains apparently separate perspectives and ignorance? Describing the world as maya must do more than relabel the appearance needing explanation.

Buddhist aggregates and no-self

Early Buddhist analysis divides the person into five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. None is permanent, fully controllable, or a self; no independent owner is found beyond them.

The no-self argument is not simple eliminativism. Conventional persons exist as dependently originated causal processes. Actions have consequences, suffering occurs, and cultivation changes future states without an unchanging soul.

The challenge is continuity and ownership. How can memory, responsibility, and rebirth connect stages without identity? Buddhist accounts appeal to conditioned causal series, while opponents argue this cannot support the first-person unity presupposed by memory.

The modern comparison with bundle theory is helpful but incomplete. No-self is also a practical diagnosis: attachment to a permanent ego produces suffering.

Momentariness

Some Buddhist schools hold that conditioned phenomena exist only momentarily. Apparent endurance is rapid causal succession rather than one thing persisting.

Momentariness intensifies the no-self problem. A cognition at one moment cannot literally be the same state at the next; memory and agency must be transmitted causally.

The view anticipates stage and process ontologies but is tied to doctrines of dependent origination and impermanence. Its arguments concern causal efficacy and change, not only the geometry of time.

Reflexive awareness

Indian and Tibetan Buddhist debates ask whether cognition is intrinsically aware of itself. Reflexive awareness (svasamvedana) is invoked to explain how experiences are known without a second cognition and regress.

Defenders argue that a cognition illuminates itself and its object as a lamp illuminates objects and itself. Opponents, especially Madhyamaka critics, deny an intrinsically self-revealing mental essence and explain self-knowledge through later or relational processes.

The dispute parallels contemporary higher-order versus first-order theories. Is self-awareness built into experience, supplied by another representation, or constructed through interpretation? The conceptual frameworks differ, but the regress problem is genuinely shared.

Yogacara

Yogacara analyses experience through consciousness, representation, and karmic conditioning. It is often labelled "mind-only," but interpretations range from metaphysical idealism to a methodological critique of subject-object reification.

The storehouse consciousness (alayavijnana) is proposed in some texts to explain continuity, latent dispositions, and karmic seeds without a permanent self. It functions as a causal stream, not an immutable owner.

Yogacara's analysis of constructed subject-object duality bears on representationalism and self-model theories. Translating it directly into modern idealism can obscure its epistemic and practical purpose: overcoming mistaken reification.

Madhyamaka and emptiness

Madhyamaka argues that persons and mental states are empty of independent intrinsic nature because they arise dependently. Emptiness is not another substance and is itself empty.

This challenges both a permanent self and any final mental atom from which the self is constructed. Conventional explanations remain valid without an ultimate bearer.

The view is not equivalent to physicalism or eliminativism. It rejects the demand that either mind or matter possess self-grounding essence. The two-truths framework separates conventional person-talk from ultimate analysis.

Chinese heart-mind

Classical Chinese xin is often translated as heart-mind. The term resists a sharp division between cognition and emotion. Thinking, feeling, desire, and moral orientation belong to one cultivated capacity.

This challenges a taxonomy in which reason and emotion are independent faculties and morality is imposed on desire from outside. Attention and affect shape how situations are perceived as calling for action.

Translation matters. Rendering xin simply as "mind" can import a private inner realm; rendering it as "heart" can suggest mere feeling. Heart-mind marks their practical unity.

Confucian cultivation

Confucian traditions understand persons relationally through family, ritual, role, and moral cultivation. The self is not an isolated substance with social properties added later; practical identity develops within patterned relationships.

Mencius emphasises affective sprouts of virtue, such as compassion, that can be cultivated. Xunzi stresses transformation through ritual, learning, and deliberate practice. Both connect emotion, attention, and agency with social formation while disagreeing about native tendencies.

The framework supports relational and narrative accounts of self without denying bodily or psychological individuality. It also warns against treating autonomy as independence from every social source.

Daoist transformation

Daoist texts challenge rigid distinctions, fixed perspectives, and over-controlled action. Wu wei names effortless or non-forcing action responsive to situations, not literal inactivity.

Zhuangzi's transformation stories and skill examples question a fixed ego and celebrate fluid perspective. Skilled action can exceed explicit rule-following, anticipating themes in embodied cognition.

These similarities should not erase Daoist ethical and cosmological aims. The point is not a computational hypothesis but a challenge to reflective control as the sole model of intelligence and agency.

What is mind's boundary?individual self; organismic process; dependent series; relational heart-mind
What is liberation or flourishing?true knowledge of self; discrimination; non-dual realisation; release from self-grasping; cultivated harmony

The rows cross rather than align into one Western spectrum. A view can affirm fundamental consciousness while denying individual substantial selves, or affirm an enduring self while treating cognition as episodic properties.

Methodological lessons

Comparative philosophy should preserve arguments and conceptual roles rather than match words by surface resemblance. Atman, purusha, vijnana, and xin are not variants of one item called mind.

At the same time, refusing comparison entirely prevents mutual criticism. Questions about memory, ownership, regress, unity, causal continuity, embodiment, and self- awareness create real points of contact.

The productive method states the local problem, reconstructs the argument in its own framework, and only then compares explanatory costs.
