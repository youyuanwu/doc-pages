Key Figures in Philosophy of Mind

A compact reference to figures whose arguments are used in this section. The entries identify a contribution rather than summarise a career. The changing problem structure is developed in the history page.

Ancient, medieval, and comparative traditions

  • Plato (c. 428-348 BCE) - the separable soul, tripartite psychology, recollection, and arguments connecting mind with immortality.
  • Aristotle (384-322 BCE) - soul as the form and actuality of a living body; graded capacities of nutrition, perception, movement, and intellect; a precursor to embodied and functional views.
  • Avicenna / Ibn Sina (c. 980-1037) - the Flying Man argument for self-awareness independent of bodily awareness.
  • Aquinas (1225-1274) - hylomorphic soul-body unity with a disputed route to the intellect's subsistence; see substance dualism.
  • Gangesa (c. 13th-14th c.) and the Nyaya tradition - enduring self, cognition as its property, and realist theories of knowledge; see comparative traditions.
  • Kapila / the Samkhya tradition - purusha as inactive witness-consciousness distinct from natural mental processes.
  • Shankara (traditionally 8th c.) - Advaita non-dualism and the identity of Atman and Brahman.
  • Vasubandhu (c. 4th-5th c.) - Buddhist analyses of aggregates, cognition, and Yogacara representation.
  • Nagarjuna (c. 2nd-3rd c.) - dependent origination and emptiness as critique of self-grounding persons and mental states.
  • Confucius (551-479 BCE), Mencius (c. 372-289 BCE), and Xunzi (c. 310-235 BCE) - heart-mind, affective cultivation, ritual, and relational personhood.
  • Zhuangzi (c. 4th c. BCE) - changing perspectives, self-transformation, and skilled action beyond explicit rule-following.

Early modern and Enlightenment

  • Descartes (1596-1650) - the cogito, mind as thinking substance, real distinction, and the interaction problem.
  • Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618-1680) - the decisive challenge to how an unextended mind can move an extended body.
  • Spinoza (1632-1677) - one substance under mental and physical attributes, avoiding interaction through parallel expression.
  • Leibniz (1646-1716) - perception in monads, pre-established harmony, and the mill argument against finding perception among mechanical parts.
  • Locke (1632-1704) - consciousness and memory in personal identity; experience and reflection as sources of ideas.
  • Hume (1711-1776) - association and the bundle theory of the self.
  • Kant (1724-1804) - transcendental unity of apperception and the subject as a condition of unified experience rather than an introspected substance.

Psychology and phenomenology

  • Brentano (1838-1917) - intentionality as the mark of the mental; see aboutness.
  • William James (1842-1910) - the stream of consciousness, attention, habit, and radical empiricism.
  • Husserl (1859-1938) - phenomenological reduction, intentional structure, noesis and noema, and time-consciousness; see Phenomenology.
  • Freud (1856-1939) - dynamically unconscious wishes and conflict; see Unconscious Mental States.
  • Heidegger (1889-1976) - being-in-the-world, practical significance, and the ready-to-hand.
  • Merleau-Ponty (1908-1961) - the lived body, body schema, motor intentionality, and perceptual world-involvement.
  • Sartre (1905-1980) - pre-reflective self-consciousness and consciousness as world-directed rather than an inner object.

Behaviour, identity, and function

  • Watson (1878-1958) - methodological behaviourism and psychology as public study.
  • Ryle (1900-1976) - category mistakes, multi-track dispositions, and the critique of the Ghost in the Machine; see Behaviourism.
  • Skinner (1904-1990) - radical behaviourism, reinforcement, and environmental explanation.
  • Place (1924-2000), Smart (1920-2012), and Feigl (1902-1988) - the modern mind-brain identity theory.
  • Putnam (1926-2016) - machine functionalism, multiple realisation, and Twin Earth externalism.
  • Lewis (1941-2001) - Ramsey-Lewis functional definition and the ability response to Mary.
  • Block (b. 1942) - absent qualia, the China brain, access versus phenomenal consciousness, overflow, and Inverted Earth.
  • Fodor (1935-2017) - language of thought, modularity, asymmetric dependence, and the challenge from systematicity to connectionism.

Action, content, and mind

  • Davidson (1917-2003) - reasons as causes, anomalous monism, radical interpretation, and mental anomalism.
  • Anscombe (1919-2001) - intention, practical knowledge, and action under a description; see Reasons, Intentions, and Action.
  • Bratman (b. 1945) - intentions as partial plans coordinating bounded agents.
  • Dretske (1932-2013) - informational semantics and naturalised representation.
  • Millikan (b. 1933) - teleosemantics, proper function, and producer-consumer structure; see Naturalizing Content.
  • Searle (b. 1932) - biological naturalism, original intentionality, and the Chinese room.
  • Dennett (1942-2024) - intentional stance, heterophenomenology, real patterns, multiple drafts, and the deflationary approach to qualia.

Consciousness and the physical

  • Nagel (b. 1937) - subjectivity and the what-it-is-like criterion; see the detailed bat essay.
  • Jackson (b. 1942) - Mary's knowledge argument and epiphenomenal qualia, followed by a later return to physicalism.
  • Levine (b. 1952) - the explanatory gap.
  • Chalmers (b. 1966) - zombies, the hard problem, two-dimensional argument, organisational invariance, and panpsychist alternatives.
  • McGinn (b. 1950) - cognitive closure and mysterianism.
  • Frankish (b. 1962) - illusionism as a positive theory of why introspection attributes qualia.
  • Kim (1934-2019) - supervenience, realisation, and the causal exclusion challenge to non-reductive physicalism.
  • Goff (b. 1977) and Strawson (b. 1952) - contemporary panpsychism and realistic monism.

Cognitive science and situated mind

  • Turing (1912-1954) - machine intelligence framed through behavioural conversation rather than an essence of thinking.
  • Newell (1927-1992) and Simon (1916-2001) - physical symbol systems and computational cognitive architecture.
  • Marr (1945-1980) - computational, algorithmic, and implementational levels of explanation.
  • Rumelhart (1942-2011) and McClelland (b. 1948) - parallel distributed processing and learned representation.
  • Gibson (1904-1979) - ecological information and affordances.
  • Varela (1946-2001), Thompson (b. 1962), and Rosch (b. 1938) - embodied mind, enactivism, and dialogue with Buddhist philosophy.
  • Clark (b. 1957) - predictive processing, embodiment, and the extended mind.
  • Chalmers and Clark - the parity argument and Otto's notebook.
  • Dehaene (b. 1965) - global neuronal workspace and experimental study of access.
  • Lamme (b. 1959) - recurrent processing and phenomenal overflow.
  • Tononi (b. 1960) - integrated information theory.
  • Graziano (b. 1967) - attention-schema theory and the meta-problem of awareness.

Self, persons, and non-human minds

  • Parfit (1942-2017) - fission, reductionism about persons, and identity not being what matters.
  • Frankfurt (1929-2023) - hierarchical desire, identification, and guidance.
  • Gazzaniga (b. 1939) - split-brain research and the interpreter hypothesis.
  • Schechter - philosophical analysis of self-consciousness and split brains.
  • Clark and Chalmers - external cognitive scaffolds as possible constituents rather than mere aids.
  • de Waal (1948-2024) and Godfrey-Smith (b. 1965) - comparative cognition and alternatives to human-centred evidence for animal minds.

How to use this page

Names do not settle arguments. A label such as "functionalism" or "phenomenology" covers several claims, and many figures revised their positions. Follow the linked issue page for premises, objections, and current alternatives; use this page to locate the source and the claim for which it matters.
