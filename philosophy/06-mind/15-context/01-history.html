A History of Philosophy of Mind

The history of philosophy of mind is not a sequence of answers to one fixed question. The question itself changed. Ancient accounts asked what makes a living thing alive and capable of perception; Cartesian philosophy separated thinking substance from extended matter; empiricism asked what introspection reveals; nineteenth-century psychology made mind an empirical subject; behaviourism sought public criteria; the cognitive revolution restored internal representation; contemporary debate divided cognition from phenomenal consciousness.

This page follows those changes in problem formulation. Figures appear where their arguments still structure live positions, not as a complete chronology.

Soul as the principle of life

For much ancient thought, psyche or soul was not primarily an immaterial consciousness. It was the principle of life. Plants nourish and reproduce, animals perceive and move, and rational animals think.

Plato often treats the soul as separable from body and internally divided among reason, spirit, and appetite. Arguments from recollection, affinity, and the soul's simplicity connect mind with immortality and moral order.

Aristotle's De Anima rejects the picture of soul as a second object inhabiting a body. Soul is the form or actuality of a living organised body. Sight is the capacity of an eye functioning within an animal, not an item separable like a sailor from a ship. This hylomorphic approach anticipates embodied and functional views while retaining disputed claims about intellect.

The ancient contrast is therefore not simply materialism versus dualism. It is between soul as independently existing subject and soul as organisation of life.

Late antique and medieval inheritances

Augustine develops first-person reflection, memory, will, and inner temporality within a Christian framework. The mind's presence to itself becomes evidence of an immaterial soul and a route toward God.

Islamic philosophers transform Aristotle. Avicenna's Flying Man imagines a person created suspended without sensory contact who would still affirm their own existence. The argument aims to show self-awareness does not depend conceptually on awareness of the body.

Aquinas develops hylomorphism: the human soul is the substantial form of the body, not an independently complete person, while intellect supplies a route to subsistence after death. Medieval theories of inner senses, intention, and cognition are richer than the later slogan that the period merely believed in souls.

The Cartesian turn

Descartes reorganises the field around two substances. Mind is essentially thinking; body is essentially extended. The certainty of the cogito and conceivability of mind without body support distinction.

The gain is a sharp conception of consciousness and first-person knowledge. The cost is the interaction problem: how can an unextended mind affect a body? Princess Elisabeth's objection exposes the causal burden immediately.

The Cartesian formulation remains the default problem even for views rejecting its ontology. Identity theory, functionalism, and property dualism are answers to a demand created by separating subjective thought from mechanistic nature.

Rationalists and the unity of mind

Spinoza replaces interaction with one substance described under thought and extension. Mental and physical are parallel attributes rather than causal strangers. Leibniz's monads possess perception and appetite, with apparent interaction coordinated through pre-established harmony.

These views anticipate dual-aspect and panpsychist alternatives. They avoid local mind-body interaction by placing both within a more general metaphysics.

Their cost is explanatory distance from empirical mechanism. Coordination is secured by the system rather than derived from observable causal exchange.

Empiricism and association

Locke treats the mind through ideas acquired from sensation and reflection. Personal identity follows continuity of consciousness rather than substance alone, making memory central.

Hume's introspection finds perceptions but no self beyond the bundle. Causal and personal unity become products of association, resemblance, memory, and imagination.

Associationism offers a proto-psychology: complex thought arises through lawful links among simpler elements. It anticipates learning theory and connectionism, though its atomic impressions understate structured perception and active cognition.

The empirical question shifts from what soul exists to what experience contains and how mental order is formed.

Kant and the conditions of experience

Kant rejects both rationalist proof of a soul-substance and Humean derivation of unity from a mere sequence. The transcendental unity of apperception is a condition for representations to count as one subject's experience: the "I think" must be able to accompany them.

This is not empirical discovery of an ego. It is a structural requirement on unified judgment and experience. Kant thereby separates the subject as condition of experience from the self as an object known through inner sense.

The distinction anticipates minimal-self and functional-unity views while warning that introspection cannot reveal a substantial soul.

Brentano and intentionality

Brentano restores intentionality as the mark of mental phenomena: presentation, judgment, love, and hate are directed toward objects. The object need not exist, creating the problem of intentional inexistence.

The problem of mind now includes aboutness, not only consciousness or substance. Husserl develops intentional analysis through phenomenology, while Meinong responds ontologically with objects beyond existence.

Contemporary causal, teleological, inferential, and phenomenal-intentionality theories remain responses to the question Brentano sharpened. The general account is in Intentionality and Aboutness.

James and the stream

William James describes consciousness as personal, continuous, selective, and always changing - a stream, not a sequence of atomistic states. Attention and habit connect phenomenology with psychology and action.

James also develops a radical empiricism and a neutral-monist tendency: experience may be more basic than a division into mental and physical. His work resists both substance dualism and a reductive associationism.

The stream metaphor still shapes debates over temporal consciousness and unity, while its apparent continuity is tested by distributed and discrete mechanisms.

Phenomenology

Husserl introduces a disciplined first-person method focused on intentional structure, time-consciousness, embodiment, and the constitution of objects. Heidegger shifts from detached consciousness to practical being-in-the-world. Merleau-Ponty makes the lived body the subject's mode of access; Sartre analyses pre-reflective self-consciousness.

This line challenges the assumption that mind begins as an inner representation facing an external world. World-involvement, bodily skill, and perspective are prior to theoretical reflection.

The next page develops the method and its current relation to enactive and representational approaches.

Behaviourism

Early twentieth-century psychology rejected introspection as an unreliable foundation. Watson and Skinner focused on observable behaviour, learning history, and environmental control. Logical behaviourists such as Ryle analysed mental language through dispositions and attacked the "Ghost in the Machine."

Behaviourism established public standards and exposed homuncular explanation. It failed as a complete theory because behaviour can be suppressed or simulated, mental states interact holistically, and phenomenal character appears not to be a disposition.

Its methodological legacy survived: inner states must earn explanatory roles through prediction and intervention. The detailed assessment is in Behaviourism.

Identity theory

Place, Smart, and Feigl proposed that sensations are brain processes, treating the identity as an empirical discovery analogous to scientific reductions. This restored inner episodes without restoring dual substances.

Multiple realisation challenged type identity. A mental kind such as pain may occur in physically diverse organisms, making one neural type too narrow. Token identity survived more readily than reduction of psychological kinds.

The episode marks philosophy of mind's closer integration with neuroscience and its recognition that causal dependence alone does not decide whether the relation is identity, realisation, or grounding.

The cognitive revolution

Computational psychology reintroduced internal representations as explanatory states. Turing, Newell, Simon, Chomsky, Fodor, and others showed how formally structured systems could explain productivity, planning, and language.

Functionalism individuated mental states by causal role and made multiple realisation a central argument. Mind became software-like organisation rather than one material kind.

Connectionism later challenged explicit rules with distributed learned representations. The debate shifted from whether inner states may be scientific to which formats and architectures explain cognition.

The return of consciousness

Behaviourism and early cognitive science often bracketed phenomenal consciousness. Nagel's bat, Jackson's Mary, Levine's explanatory gap, and Chalmers' hard problem made subjectivity central again.

The new anti-physicalist arguments did not simply revive Cartesian substance. Property dualism, panpsychism, Russellian monism, and phenomenal-concept physicalism all respond to the gap differently.

At the same time, consciousness science developed theories of global broadcast, higher-order representation, recurrent processing, integration, and self-models. The field now combines empirical mechanism with unresolved metaphysical interpretation.

Embodiment, extension, and action

Late twentieth-century phenomenology, ecological psychology, dynamical systems, and enactivism challenged brain-bound representation. Cognition was re-situated in bodies, tools, environments, and social practices.

The extended-mind debate then asked whether external scaffolds merely aid or partly constitute cognition. Philosophy of action emphasised guidance and practical knowledge rather than an inner command followed by movement.

These developments return, by a different route, to Aristotle's organised living body and pragmatist accounts of habit while using contemporary cognitive science.

The contemporary field

Current philosophy of mind is organised less by one dualism than by crossing questions:

  • Is consciousness reducible, fundamental, or misdescribed?
  • Is content internal, external, functional, biological, or phenomenal?
  • Are cognitive processes computational, dynamical, embodied, or extended?
  • Which levels are causally explanatory?
  • What evidence warrants minds in animals and machines?
  • Is the self a subject, organism, model, narrative, or dependent process?

The field's progress consists partly in discovering that these questions do not share one answer. A functional theory of belief can coexist with a non-functional theory of experience; embodied perception can use internal generative models; content can be external while phenomenal character is narrow.

The recurring shape

Three transitions recur. A substance question becomes a property question; a property question becomes an explanatory-level question; and an apparent unity divides into capacities that can dissociate. Each transition preserves the original phenomenon while making the alternatives more precise.

History matters because discarded positions often leave constraints behind. Behaviourism failed, but public evidence remains necessary. Type identity weakened, but physical dependence remains. Introspection lost infallibility, but first-person authority remains an explanandum. The contemporary map is built from these qualified inheritances.
